Urology education

Urinary tract infections (UTIs)

Bladder and kidney infections: how to tell if it’s really a UTI, where to get care in Ontario, what treatment involves, and what helps when infections keep coming back.

Symptoms make the diagnosis, not a test alone. Burning, needing to pee often and urgently, and pain low in the belly point to a bladder infection. Cloudy or smelly urine, or a positive urine test without symptoms, usually needs no antibiotics.
Fever or back pain is different. A UTI with fever, chills, pain in your side or vomiting can be a kidney infection. It needs to be checked today, at an emergency department. Call 9-1-1 if you feel very unwell.
Infections that keep coming back can be prevented. Vaginal estrogen after menopause, cranberry, more water, methenamine and low-dose antibiotics can all help, with differing strengths of evidence. A urine culture during each infection is the first step.

Fever or shaking chills, pain in your side or back, nausea or vomiting, new confusion, or feeling very unwell? Don’t wait. See when to get help urgently.

Part 1

What a UTI is

A urinary tract infection (UTI) happens when germs, usually bacteria from your own bowel, get into the urinary tract and cause inflammation. Most UTIs are in the bladder. A few travel up to the kidneys.

UTIs are very common. About half of all women have at least one in their lifetime, and about 1 in 4 women who have one will have another within 6 months. They are less common in men, because the tube from the bladder (the urethra) is longer.

Bladder infection (cystitis)

The most common kind. It is uncomfortable but not dangerous for most people, and it usually clears quickly with the right treatment.

  • burning or pain when you pee;
  • needing to pee often, or suddenly and urgently, often passing only a little;
  • pain or pressure low in your belly, above the pubic bone;
  • sometimes blood in the urine (pink, red or cola-coloured);
  • sometimes new leaking of urine.

Kidney infection (pyelonephritis)

Less common but more serious. It needs to be checked the same day, at an emergency department (see urgent help).

  • fever of 38°C or higher, or shaking chills;
  • pain in your side or back, just below the ribs (often on one side);
  • nausea or vomiting;
  • feeling generally very unwell;
  • often, but not always, bladder symptoms too.

Why UTIs happen

In women the urethra is short and close to the vagina and anus, so bacteria can reach the bladder more easily. Things that make UTIs more likely include:

  • sex, especially frequent sex or a new partner (this is nobody’s fault, and it isn’t a sexually transmitted infection);
  • spermicide, including spermicide-coated condoms and diaphragms;
  • menopause, because lower estrogen changes the healthy bacteria in the vagina;
  • not emptying the bladder fully, for example from a prolapse, an enlarged prostate, or nerve problems;
  • kidney stones, a urinary catheter, or a recent procedure on the urinary tract;
  • pregnancy, diabetes that isn’t well controlled, and a family tendency (women whose mothers had many UTIs are more likely to have them).

Most UTIs in otherwise healthy women don’t mean anything is wrong with the bladder or kidneys.

Part 2

What isn’t a UTI

Many people are given antibiotics they don’t need. Antibiotics can cause diarrhea, thrush (a yeast infection), allergic reactions and, rarely, a serious bowel infection called C. difficile. Each course also makes future infections harder to treat.

Cloudy, dark or smelly urine on its own

Urine changes colour and smell with how much you drink, what you eat, vitamins and medicines. Without burning, urgency, belly pain or fever, it is not a sign of infection and doesn’t need a urine test. Drink more water and see if it clears.

Bacteria in the urine without symptoms

Bacteria often live in the bladder without causing harm. Doctors call this asymptomatic bacteriuria (“bacteria in the urine, no symptoms”). A dipstick or culture can be positive, yet you feel well.

It is common: between 1 in 4 and 1 in 2 older women living in long-term care have it, and almost everyone with a long-term catheter does. Treating it with antibiotics doesn’t prevent infections or help you feel better, and it causes side effects and resistant germs. So guidelines say don’t test for it and don’t treat it.

The two exceptions
  • Pregnancy. Bacteria in the urine raises the chance of a kidney infection and early labour, so pregnant people are tested once early in pregnancy and treated if it is found.
  • Before some urology procedures that cut or scrape the lining of the urinary tract, such as surgery on the prostate. Your surgical team will test and treat beforehand if needed.

Confusion or falls in an older person, without urinary symptoms

It is a common belief that a UTI is the cause when an older relative becomes muddled. Often it isn’t. Dehydration, constipation, poor sleep, pain, a new medicine, low oxygen or another infection are more likely. A positive urine test in this situation is often just bacteria that were already there.

Sudden new confusion still needs urgent care, because it can be a sign of a serious illness: go to emergency, or call 9-1-1 if the person is very drowsy or unwell. Ask the team to look for all the possible causes, not just the urine. For confusion that has built up slowly, or is the person’s usual pattern, see their own doctor soon.

Other things that feel like a UTI

If you keep having “UTI” symptoms but your urine cultures are negative (no bacteria grow), something else may be the cause. Common ones are:

  • vaginal infections or irritation (yeast, bacterial vaginosis, soaps, wipes, sprays);
  • sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia or gonorrhea, which can cause burning in any sex;
  • thinning and dryness of the vagina and urethra after menopause, which can cause burning and urgency;
  • overactive bladder, bladder pain syndrome (interstitial cystitis) or tight pelvic floor muscles;
  • kidney stones, and, rarely, other bladder problems. That is why blood in the urine between infections should always be checked (see when to see a urologist).

Repeated antibiotics won’t help these. Ask your doctor to look for the cause. For men with pelvic pain and negative cultures, see chronic pelvic pain in men.

Part 3

Where should I get care?

In Ontario you can get help for a UTI from a pharmacist, your family doctor or nurse practitioner, a walk-in or urgent care clinic, Health811, or an emergency department. Which one is right depends on your symptoms and your health.

This is a guide, not a diagnosis. It is for adults, and it errs on the side of caution. If you are worried, or you get worse at any point, get help sooner than it suggests.

The quick guide

  1. Emergency department now, or call 9-1-1

    If you have UTI symptoms with: a fever of 38°C or higher or shaking chills; pain in your side or back; nausea or vomiting; feeling very unwell, faint or very drowsy; new confusion; you can’t pass urine, or your catheter has stopped draining; heavy bleeding or clots. Call 9-1-1 if someone is very unwell, hard to wake or can’t get to hospital safely. Pregnant with contractions, bleeding or leaking fluid: go to obstetrical triage (Labour and Delivery).

  2. A doctor or nurse practitioner today

    If you: are a man with UTI symptoms; are pregnant; have a catheter, stent, kidney stones, kidney disease or a recent urinary procedure; have a weakened immune system; or are not clearly better after 2 to 3 days of antibiotics. Call your family doctor’s office for a same-day visit, or go to an urgent care or walk-in clinic. If you are pregnant, call your prenatal care provider; if you can’t reach them, go to obstetrical triage (Labour and Delivery). Fever in any of these situations means emergency.

  3. Your family doctor or nurse practitioner within a day or two

    If you: have had 2 or more UTIs in 6 months or 3 in a year; had symptoms come back within 4 weeks of treatment; or see any blood in your urine. Ask for a urine culture before any antibiotic. If you can’t be seen that soon, a walk-in clinic can do the test. If you have a self-start plan, follow it.

  4. A pharmacist, or your family doctor

    If you: are a woman who isn’t pregnant, with typical bladder symptoms (burning, needing to go often or urgently, pain low in the belly), no blood you can see in your urine, and none of the above. Ontario pharmacists can assess you and, if it is right for you, prescribe treatment.

UTIs at the pharmacy

Pharmacists in Ontario can assess and treat a simple bladder infection. The assessment is free with an Ontario health card, in person or by phone or video at many pharmacies. The antibiotic itself is paid for by you, unless the Ontario Drug Benefit or a private plan covers it. Call ahead to ask if a pharmacist is available.

The pharmacist will ask about your symptoms and health. They will send you to a doctor or nurse practitioner instead if, for example, you are male or pregnant; have fever, chills, side or back pain, nausea or vomiting; have confusion or blood in your urine that you can see; have a catheter, kidney stones, kidney disease or another urinary tract problem; have a weakened immune system; had symptoms come back within 4 weeks of antibiotics; or have had 2 UTIs in 6 months or 3 in a year.

The province pays for up to 3 pharmacy UTI assessments per person in a year.

Health811

Not sure what to do, or it’s night or the weekend? Call Health811 (dial 8-1-1; TTY 711) to talk to a registered nurse, free, 24 hours a day, in many languages. You can also chat online at health811.ontario.ca. The nurse can tell you how urgent it is and where to go. Health811 can’t prescribe.

No family doctor?

A pharmacist, walk-in or urgent care clinic, or Health811 can help with a UTI. Health811 can also help you look for a family doctor or nurse practitioner. If you have infections that keep coming back, try to find one clinic that can see you each time, so your culture results are kept together.

Part 4

Tests and treatment

The urine culture

A urine culture is a lab test that grows the bacteria from your urine, names them, and shows which antibiotics work against them. Results usually take 1 to 3 days. A quick dipstick test can’t prove or rule out a UTI on its own.

For a first, simple bladder infection in a healthy woman, a culture often isn’t needed: the usual antibiotics nearly always work. A culture is important, and should be done before you start the antibiotic, if:

  • you get UTIs often (2 in 6 months or 3 in a year), or symptoms came back soon after treatment;
  • you are a man, or pregnant;
  • you might have a kidney infection, or you have a catheter or another urinary tract problem;
  • you are not getting better on an antibiotic.

If you get UTIs often, a positive culture from each infection is what proves they really are infections. It guides the right antibiotic, and it is the first thing a specialist will ask for. Ask for a copy of each result, or write it in your UTI log.

How to give a good urine sample
  1. Wash your hands. If you can, don’t pee for at least an hour beforehand.
  2. Women: hold the labia apart with one hand. Men: pull back the foreskin if you have one. Wipe with the cloth if you are given one.
  3. Start peeing into the toilet, then, without stopping, catch the middle part of the stream in the sterile cup. Finish in the toilet.
  4. Close the lid tightly without touching the inside. Label it as the lab asks.
  5. Take it to the lab or clinic as the lab instructs, usually within 2 hours. If that isn’t possible, keep it in the fridge and ask how long it can wait.

A sample contaminated with skin or vaginal bacteria may grow several germs and give a confusing result. If that happens, the test may need repeating.

Treatment of a bladder infection

Most bladder infections are treated with a short course of an antibiotic that works well in the bladder and has few side effects. In Ontario the usual choices are:

  • Nitrofurantoin (for example Macrobid), twice a day for 5 days, with food.
  • Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Septra, Bactrim) for 3 days, or trimethoprim alone for 3 days.
  • Fosfomycin (Monurol), a single dose dissolved in water.

Stronger antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin are kept for kidney infections and other serious infections, because they have more side effects and resistance to them is common.

Most people start to feel better within 1 to 2 days. Take the antibiotic exactly as prescribed. Tell the prescriber about your other medicines: some antibiotics interact with warfarin, blood pressure pills, methotrexate and others.

Easing the symptoms while the antibiotic works
  • Acetaminophen (Tylenol) or ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) can ease the pain, if they are safe for you.
  • A heating pad or hot water bottle on your lower belly may help.
  • Drink enough to keep your urine pale yellow. Drinking large amounts won’t clear the infection faster.
  • Coffee, alcohol and fizzy drinks can make burning and urgency worse for a few days.

Treatment of a kidney infection

Kidney infections need a different antibiotic, often ciprofloxacin, a cephalosporin or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, chosen according to the culture. If you are improving, treatment is usually 5 to 7 days with ciprofloxacin or a similar antibiotic, or about 7 days with others. Some people need longer, for example if the infection was in the blood or is slow to settle. Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin don’t reach the kidneys well and aren’t used. If you are vomiting, very unwell or not improving, you may need antibiotics by vein in hospital at first, and a scan to check that nothing is blocking the kidney.

Call back if

  • you aren’t clearly better after 2 to 3 days of antibiotics;
  • your symptoms come back within a few weeks of finishing;
  • the lab calls to say the bacteria are resistant to your antibiotic;
  • you get a rash, swelling, or diarrhea that is watery and frequent (3 or more times a day) or bloody;
  • you see blood in your urine that doesn’t clear when the infection does.

Go to emergency for fever, side or back pain, vomiting or feeling very unwell.

After you are better

You don’t need a repeat urine test to “make sure it’s gone” if your symptoms have cleared, unless you are pregnant or your doctor asks for one.

If you saw blood in your urine, or a test found blood, it should be checked again after the infection has cleared. Blood that is still there needs to be looked into.

Part 5

Men, pregnancy, older adults and catheters

These situations need a little more care. Open the one that applies to you.

UTIs in men

Always see a doctor or nurse practitioner, and always have a urine culture.

UTIs are much less common in men, so when one happens it is worth finding out why. Pharmacists in Ontario don’t treat UTIs in men.

What to expect
  • A urine culture before the antibiotic, and often an exam, which may include checking the prostate.
  • Treatment is usually for 7 days, longer if the prostate or kidney is involved.
  • Afterwards, a check that your bladder empties fully (a quick bladder scan or ultrasound), especially if your stream is weak or you get up often at night. An enlarged prostate is a common cause.
  • In younger men, burning is often caused by a sexually transmitted infection such as chlamydia or gonorrhea rather than a UTI. A urine test for these is simple.
Prostate infection (acute prostatitis)

A sudden bacterial infection of the prostate causes fever and chills, pain between the scrotum and the anus or low in the belly, burning, and often trouble passing urine. It can make you very ill. Go to emergency the same day. It is treated with antibiotics that reach the prostate, usually for at least 10 to 14 days and sometimes up to 4 weeks, and sometimes in hospital at first. If you can’t pass urine, you may need a catheter for a while.

Long-lasting pelvic pain with negative cultures is usually not an infection. See chronic pelvic pain in men.

When men should see a urologist

After a fever with a UTI, a kidney or prostate infection, more than one UTI, blood in the urine, a bladder that doesn’t empty fully, or kidney stones. Your family doctor can refer you.

UTIs in pregnancy

Call your prenatal care provider (doctor or midwife) the same day if you have symptoms.

In pregnancy, bladder infections can travel to the kidneys more easily, and kidney infections can lead to early labour. So UTIs are always treated, and a culture is always done.

What to expect
  • A urine culture early in pregnancy, even without symptoms. Bacteria found then are treated, unlike at other times of life.
  • Antibiotics chosen to be safe for the baby at your stage of pregnancy. Some, such as ciprofloxacin, aren’t used.
  • A repeat culture after treatment, to check the bacteria have gone.
  • Fever, side or back pain, nausea or vomiting, contractions, bleeding or leaking fluid with a UTI: go to obstetrical triage (Labour and Delivery) at the hospital where you plan to give birth, or the nearest emergency department.

Older adults and caregivers

Look for clear urinary symptoms or fever before thinking “UTI”.

Bacteria in the urine are very common in older people and usually harmless (see Part 2). Testing urine because it smells, looks cloudy, or because someone has had a fall or seems more confused, often leads to antibiotics that don’t help and can harm.

When a UTI is more likely

A UTI is more likely when there are urinary symptoms (new burning, new urgency or frequency, new leaking, pain low in the belly or in the side, blood in the urine) or a fever or shaking chills. Then a urine culture and assessment are sensible.

Sudden new confusion, with or without these, needs urgent care at an emergency department. The team should look at fluids, bowels, medicines, pain, sleep, oxygen and other infections, not just the urine. For mild changes that come on slowly, such as a little less appetite or energy, offer drinks if that is safe for the person, watch closely, and call their doctor.

If you have a urinary catheter

Bacteria are always in the urine after a few weeks. Treat only true infections.

Anyone with a catheter in place for more than a few weeks will have bacteria in the urine, and the urine may look cloudy or have sediment. This is expected and isn’t treated.

Signs of a real catheter infection
  • fever or shaking chills;
  • new pain in the side or back, or low in the belly;
  • new confusion or feeling very unwell;
  • new blood in the urine.

These need assessment the same day, and emergency care if you feel very unwell. If an infection is treated, the catheter is usually changed first (if it has been in for 2 weeks or more), and the urine sample is taken from the new catheter.

If the catheter stops draining and your bladder feels full or painful, it may be blocked: call your community nurse, or go to emergency if no one can help quickly.

Lowering the risk
  • Wash your hands before and after touching the catheter or bag.
  • Keep the bag below the level of your bladder and off the floor. Don’t let the tube kink.
  • Keep the system closed: don’t disconnect the bag from the catheter unless you have been shown how.
  • Wash around the catheter daily with soap and water.
  • Ask regularly whether the catheter is still needed. If you use intermittent (in-and-out) catheters, follow your nurse’s routine.

Antibiotics to “prevent” infections with a long-term catheter are not recommended: they don’t work, and they lead to resistant bacteria.

Part 6

Preventing UTIs that keep coming back

Recurrent UTI means 2 or more infections in 6 months, or 3 or more in a year. It is common, frustrating, and usually not a sign that anything is wrong with your bladder or kidneys. There are several ways to have fewer infections.

First

Make sure they really are infections

Give a urine sample for culture each time you have symptoms, before you start an antibiotic, and keep a record of the results. This shows whether the symptoms are caused by bacteria, which ones, and which antibiotics still work. If cultures keep coming back negative, the cause is probably something else (see Part 2), and prevention for UTIs won’t help.

Most women with recurrent UTIs don’t need a cystoscopy (a camera test of the bladder) or a CT scan. Your doctor will suggest tests if something in your history points to a cause.

Things you can start yourself

Drink more water

If you drink less than about 1.5 litres a day.

Guideline option (conditional), based on one trial

More water means more frequent peeing, which flushes bacteria out of the bladder. Aim for about 1.5 litres a day on top of what you already drink, or enough that your urine stays pale. Check with your doctor first if you have heart or kidney failure or have been told to limit fluids.

What the study found

140 women before menopause who had at least 3 UTIs in the past year and drank less than 1.5 litres a day were asked either to add 1.5 litres of water a day or to carry on as usual, for a year.

UTIs per woman over one year

Usual drinking3.2
Extra water1.7

Hooton and colleagues, JAMA Internal Medicine, 2018. The women who drank more also needed fewer antibiotics. We don’t know if extra water helps people who already drink plenty.

Cranberry

Capsules, tablets or juice, every day.

Recommended by guidelines (moderate strength)

Natural substances in cranberry make it harder for bacteria to stick to the bladder wall. It doesn’t treat an infection that has already started.

What the studies found, and how to take it

A 2023 Cochrane review of 50 trials found that cranberry products lowered the chance of a culture-proven UTI. In women with recurrent UTIs, infections were about a quarter less likely. Cranberry did not seem to help older people living in care homes, pregnant women, or people whose bladders don’t empty because of nerve problems.

  • Products vary a lot. Choose one whose label says how much of the active ingredient (“PACs”) it contains, and take it every day. Trials used different products and doses, so there is no single proven dose.
  • Juice works too, but it can add a lot of sugar. Choose unsweetened or low-sugar juice.
  • Side effects are uncommon, mostly an upset stomach. If you take warfarin, have had kidney stones, or have diabetes, ask your pharmacist first.

Avoid spermicide

If your infections are linked to sex.

Consistent evidence from observational studies

Spermicide (in some condoms, gels, foams and with diaphragms) upsets the healthy bacteria in the vagina and is one of the clearest risk factors for UTIs. If you use it, ask about other birth control. The pill, IUDs and condoms without spermicide don’t raise the risk.

D-mannose

A sugar sold as a supplement.

The largest trial found no clear benefit

D-mannose was thought to stop bacteria sticking to the bladder, and early small studies looked promising. A large, well-run trial in 2024 found that it didn’t clearly prevent UTIs. The 2025 urology guideline (AUA, CUA and SUFU) now says D-mannose on its own may not work. It is unlikely to do harm, but it shouldn’t replace treatments that work.

What the trial found

598 women in the UK with recurrent UTIs took 2 g of D-mannose or a dummy powder daily for 6 months. A UTI needing medical care happened in about 51 in 100 women taking D-mannose and 56 in 100 taking the dummy powder, a difference that could easily be down to chance.

Hayward and colleagues (MERIT trial), JAMA Internal Medicine, 2024.

Probiotics

“Good bacteria” as capsules or vaginal products.

Weak evidence so far

Restoring the vagina’s healthy Lactobacillus bacteria makes sense, and some small trials of specific strains look promising, but reviews haven’t found a clear benefit yet. They are generally safe if you want to try them, but don’t rely on them alone.

Treatments to discuss with your doctor

Vaginal estrogen

For women around or after menopause.

Recommended by Canadian, American and European guidelines

After menopause, lower estrogen thins the tissues of the vagina and urethra and reduces the healthy bacteria that keep germs away. A small dose of estrogen placed in the vagina reverses this. It is one of the most effective ways to prevent UTIs after menopause, and it often eases dryness, burning and urgency too.

What the studies found

In the best-known trial, 93 women after menopause with recurrent UTIs used a vaginal estrogen cream (estriol) or a dummy cream for 8 months. Estriol cream isn’t sold in Canada; the vaginal products here contain estradiol or conjugated estrogens, which are expected to work in the same way.

UTIs per woman per year

Dummy cream5.9
Vaginal estrogen0.5

Raz and Stamm, New England Journal of Medicine, 1993. Later reviews confirm the benefit for vaginal estrogen, but not for estrogen taken as pills.

Using it, and is it safe?
  • It comes as a cream, a small tablet or insert, or a soft ring that stays in place for 3 months. Most are used every night for 2 weeks, then twice a week.
  • The dose is very low and very little reaches the blood. It is not the same as hormone replacement pills, and you don’t need to take progesterone with it.
  • It works only while you use it, so plan to keep going.
  • If you have had breast or uterine cancer, it may still be an option: talk to your cancer team. Tell your doctor about any vaginal bleeding after menopause.

Methenamine hippurate

A daily non-antibiotic tablet (Hiprex).

Guideline option, supported by a large trial

Methenamine turns into a mild antiseptic in the bladder, so bacteria can’t grow well there. Because it isn’t an antibiotic, bacteria don’t become resistant to it. It is taken twice a day, usually for 6 to 12 months or longer. It needs a prescription.

What the study found

The ALTAR trial in the UK compared methenamine with daily low-dose antibiotics in 240 women with recurrent UTIs for a year.

UTIs per woman per year, while taking it

Daily antibiotic0.89
Methenamine1.38

Daily antibiotics prevented slightly more infections (about one more every two years). But methenamine worked nearly as well without using antibiotics, which the researchers judged an acceptable trade-off. Side effects were similar and mostly mild.

Harding and colleagues, BMJ, 2022. Methenamine hasn’t been well studied in people with catheters or bladder emptying problems.

Things to know
  • Possible side effects: upset stomach or nausea (taking it with food helps), and rarely a rash.
  • It isn’t suitable if you have significant kidney or liver disease. Some medicines and antacids stop it working: ask your pharmacist.
  • It prevents infections. It doesn’t treat one that has already started.
  • Check whether your drug plan covers it.

One antibiotic dose after sex

If your UTIs usually start within a day or two of sex.

Works well in trials

You take a single small dose of an antibiotic within about 2 hours after sex. It works about as well as a daily antibiotic for women whose infections are linked to sex, with much less antibiotic overall.

What the study found

UTIs per woman per year

Dummy pill3.6
Antibiotic after sex0.3

Stapleton and colleagues, JAMA, 1990: a small trial of 27 women using trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole after sex for 6 months.

A low-dose antibiotic every day

Usually at bedtime, for 6 to 12 months.

Works well while you take it

A small daily dose of an antibiotic such as nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim or cephalexin. While you take it, infections become much less frequent: in trials, about 4 in 5 fewer. When you stop, the infections often come back at the same rate as before, so it can be a way to break a cycle rather than a cure.

The trade-offs
  • Side effects are possible: stomach upset, thrush, rash.
  • Infections that do happen are more likely to be resistant to that antibiotic. A culture is important for any breakthrough infection.
  • Long-term nitrofurantoin rarely causes lung, liver or nerve problems. Tell your doctor about a new cough or shortness of breath, yellow skin, or numbness or tingling.
  • Your doctor will choose the antibiotic based on your past culture results and review it at least every 6 to 12 months.

Self-start antibiotics

A prescription you keep at home and start when symptoms begin.

Supported by guidelines

For women whose recurrent UTIs have been confirmed by culture, and who recognise the symptoms well, this means faster relief and fewer clinic visits, and you take antibiotics only when you need them. Studies show women in this situation are usually right about when they have a UTI.

How it usually works
  1. Your doctor gives you a prescription for a short course and, ideally, a “standing” lab requisition: a lab form you can reuse to drop off a urine sample for culture each time, without a new visit.
  2. When typical symptoms start, give a urine sample for culture first, then start the antibiotic.
  3. Call your doctor if you aren’t clearly better in 2 to 3 days, if symptoms are different from usual, or if the culture shows a resistant germ.
  4. Never use it for fever, side or back pain, or vomiting: get seen the same day.

What about wiping, peeing after sex and tight clothes?

You may have been told to wipe front to back, pee right after sex, avoid baths, tight underwear or hot tubs, or never hold your urine. These tips are harmless, and you are welcome to keep any that feel right to you. But when researchers compared several hundred women aged 18 to 30 with and without recurrent UTIs, none of these habits made a difference. The things that did were how often people had sex, using spermicide, a new partner, having a first UTI at a young age, and having a mother with frequent UTIs.

Your infections are not caused by poor hygiene. Washing more, douching or using special products can irritate the skin and upset the vagina’s healthy bacteria, so it is best avoided. Treating constipation is sensible, even though the evidence is thin.

When recurrent UTIs need a urologist

Most recurrent UTIs can be managed well by your family doctor or nurse practitioner. Ask to be referred to a urologist if you have:

  • blood in your urine between infections, when you have no symptoms;
  • kidney infections, especially more than one, or a UTI with fever;
  • UTIs as a man, especially more than one;
  • kidney stones, now or in the past, or infections with germs linked to stones (such as Proteus);
  • the same germ coming back soon after the right treatment;
  • a bladder that doesn’t empty fully, a weak stream, or a prolapse (a bulge in the vagina);
  • air or stool in your urine, or infections with a mix of bowel germs, which can mean a connection between the bowel and bladder;
  • previous surgery on your bladder, kidneys or pelvis, or nerve problems affecting your bladder;
  • infections that keep coming back despite trying the prevention options above.

Bring your UTI log to the appointment.

Part 8

When to get help urgently

Most UTIs are easily treated. A few spread to the kidneys or the blood, which can become serious quickly. Know the signs.

Go to an emergency department now if you have

  • fever of 38°C or higher, or shaking chills, with urinary symptoms or pain in your side or back;
  • pain in your side or back, or nausea or vomiting, with urinary symptoms, or you can’t keep fluids or antibiotics down;
  • if you are pregnant: fever, side or back pain, contractions, bleeding or leaking fluid (see below for where to go);
  • signs of a serious infection spreading in the body (sepsis): feeling very unwell, a very fast heartbeat or breathing, dizziness or fainting, cold, clammy or blotchy skin, passing very little urine, or being very drowsy or hard to wake;
  • sudden new confusion, or a sudden change in behaviour, especially in an older person (the cause may or may not be a UTI, but it needs checking right away);
  • you can’t pass urine, or your catheter has stopped draining and your bladder is full and painful;
  • heavy bleeding or clots in your urine;
  • a man with UTI symptoms and fever.

If you are pregnant, go to obstetrical triage (Labour and Delivery) at the hospital where you plan to give birth, or the nearest emergency department.

Call 9-1-1 if someone is very unwell, very drowsy or confused, or can’t get to hospital safely. In London, the adult emergency departments are at Victoria Hospital and University Hospital.

See a doctor or nurse practitioner the same day if

  • you are a man with UTI symptoms, or pregnant with UTI symptoms;
  • you are not clearly better after 2 to 3 days of antibiotics, or you are getting worse;
  • you have a catheter, stent or kidney tube and symptoms of infection, or a weakened immune system.

If it is evening or the weekend, call Health811 (dial 8-1-1) for advice from a nurse, or go to an urgent care or walk-in clinic.

Contact your family doctor soon if

  • you see blood in your urine and it doesn’t clear when the infection does, or blood is found in your urine when you have no infection;
  • you have had 2 UTIs in 6 months or 3 in a year;
  • you keep having UTI symptoms but your cultures are negative.

Part 9 · For family physicians and referring clinicians

UTI and recurrent UTI: key messages and referral

A short summary for primary care, nurse practitioners and emergency clinicians, focused on what makes a urology referral useful. Patients are welcome to read it too.

  1. Culture during a symptomatic episode, before antibiotics, whenever the patient has recurrent UTI, is male or pregnant, has suspected pyelonephritis or complicated infection, or isn’t responding. For recurrent UTI, culture-documented episodes are the diagnosis (AUA/CUA/SUFU; EAU strong). Symptoms with repeatedly negative cultures call for a different diagnosis, not more antibiotics or a cystoscopy.
  2. Don’t screen for or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria, including in older adults, people with diabetes, spinal cord injury or catheters, and kidney transplant recipients more than a month after transplant. The exceptions are pregnancy and before endourological procedures that breach the mucosa (IDSA 2019). In older adults with delirium or falls and no localizing urinary or systemic signs, look for other causes rather than treating a positive culture. Avoid “test of cure” cultures in patients who are well (pregnancy is the exception).
  3. Most women with recurrent uncomplicated cystitis don’t need cystoscopy or upper-tract imaging. Work-up is for atypical features: hematuria between infections, recurrent pyelonephritis, persistence of the same organism, urease-producing organisms or stones, obstruction or raised post-void residual, pneumaturia or fecaluria, prior urological surgery.
  4. Optimize prevention in primary care first: vaginal estrogen if peri- or postmenopausal, water if intake is under 1.5 L/day, cranberry, methenamine hippurate, post-coital or continuous low-dose prophylaxis, and self-start therapy with a standing culture requisition. For D-mannose, the AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 amendment advises telling patients that D-mannose alone may not be effective (after the negative MERIT trial, 2024); EAU 2026 still lists it as a weak option.
  5. Hematuria found during a UTI needs a repeat microscopic urinalysis after treatment. Visible hematuria outside infection, or microhematuria that persists, should be evaluated, not attributed to “another UTI”.

Urgent: call the urologist on call

  • Infection with an obstructed kidney (obstructing stone, hydronephrosis): needs decompression, not just antibiotics.
  • Renal, perinephric or prostatic abscess, or emphysematous pyelonephritis.
  • Pyelonephritis not improving after 48 to 72 hours of appropriate antibiotics: image (CT, or ultrasound) and call.
  • Urinary retention with infection or acute prostatitis.

Refer (routine or semi-urgent)

  • Visible hematuria outside an infection: cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging. Please mark as priority.
  • Persistent microhematuria (3 or more RBC/HPF on microscopy) after the UTI has cleared, at intermediate or high risk under the AUA/SUFU microhematuria guideline (2020, amended 2025).
  • Recurrent culture-proven UTI despite optimized prevention, or with any of the atypical features above.
  • Men with febrile UTI or prostatitis, recurrent UTI, raised post-void residual, hematuria, or no clear cause.
  • Suspected fistula (pneumaturia, fecaluria, recurrent polymicrobial UTI): semi-urgent.
  • Stones, especially with Proteus or other urease-producing organisms.
  • Neurogenic bladder, or recurrent UTI after urological surgery.

Usually no referral needed

  • A single uncomplicated cystitis that resolves.
  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria (don’t treat, don’t refer).
  • “Recurrent UTI” without any positive cultures: get cultures during symptoms first.
  • Recurrent uncomplicated cystitis in a woman where prevention hasn’t yet been tried.
  • Microhematuria only during a UTI that clears on repeat microscopy.

Treatment reminders
  • Acute cystitis in women (Ontario pharmacist algorithm, IDSA): nitrofurantoin macrocrystals 100 mg twice daily for 5 days; TMP-SMX 160/800 mg twice daily for 3 days (avoid if resistance is high locally or the patient had it in the last 3 months); trimethoprim 3 days; fosfomycin 3 g once (in one randomized trial, clinical resolution at 28 days was 58% with single-dose fosfomycin vs 70% with 5 days of nitrofurantoin: Huttner, JAMA 2018); pivmecillinam (Selexid, available in Canada; product monograph 2019) 400 mg three times daily for 3 to 5 days. Avoid fluoroquinolones for cystitis. Check renal function before nitrofurantoin, especially in older adults, and interactions with TMP-SMX (warfarin, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, spironolactone, methotrexate).
  • Afebrile UTI in men: culture first; 7 days was non-inferior to 14 days in a randomized trial (Drekonja, JAMA 2021). Nitrofurantoin only if prostatic involvement is unlikely. Consider STI testing in younger men.
  • Pyelonephritis and complicated UTI (IDSA 2025): if improving on effective therapy, 5 to 7 days of a fluoroquinolone or 7 days of another active agent, counted from the first day of effective therapy; step down to oral when improving. Avoid a fluoroquinolone if the patient had one in the past 12 months. Nitrofurantoin and oral fosfomycin are not appropriate.
  • Acute bacterial prostatitis and febrile UTI in men: IDSA 2025 notes that 10 to 14 days may be considered when prostatitis is suspected; EAU suggests 2 to 4 weeks for acute bacterial prostatitis. Use an agent that reaches the prostate; image for abscess if slow to respond.
  • Fluoroquinolone safety: Health Canada has warned of tendinitis and tendon rupture, aortic aneurysm and dissection, peripheral neuropathy, neuropsychiatric effects, dysglycemia, and rare disabling and potentially persistent effects. Reserve fluoroquinolones for infections where the benefit outweighs these risks, and take particular care in older adults, patients on corticosteroids, and those with aortic aneurysm or its risk factors.
  • Catheter-associated UTI: treat only with symptoms; replace a catheter in place for 2 weeks or more and culture from the new one.
Recurrent UTI prevention: evidence at a glance
  • Vaginal estrogen (peri- or postmenopausal): AUA/CUA/SUFU “should recommend” (moderate, Grade B); EAU strong. Raz and Stamm 1993 (intravaginal estriol cream, not marketed in Canada; Canadian products are estradiol or conjugated estrogens): 0.5 vs 5.9 UTIs per patient-year.
  • Methenamine hippurate 1 g twice daily: AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 conditional, Grade C (new); EAU strong in women without urinary tract abnormalities. ALTAR (BMJ 2022): 1.38 vs 0.89 episodes per person-year vs daily antibiotics, within the non-inferiority margin of 1.
  • Cranberry: AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 “should offer” (moderate, Grade B); EAU strong, with low-quality evidence noted. Cochrane 2023: RR 0.74 (0.55 to 0.99) in women with recurrent UTI; no benefit in institutionalized older adults, pregnancy or neurogenic bladder.
  • Increased water if intake is under 1.5 L/day: AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 conditional, Grade C (new; one RCT). Hooton 2018: 1.7 vs 3.2 episodes in 12 months.
  • D-mannose: inform patients that D-mannose alone may not be effective (AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025, moderate, Grade B); EAU 2026 weak, noting contradictory evidence. MERIT: 51.0% vs 55.7% medically attended UTI at 6 months, not significant.
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis: continuous (RR about 0.2 for recurrence during prophylaxis, Cochrane 2004; benefit does not persist after stopping) or post-coital. Choose by prior susceptibilities, review at 6 to 12 months, and culture breakthrough infections.
  • Self-start therapy for reliable patients with culture-documented recurrent UTI: EAU strong; AUA/CUA/SUFU supports it in selected patients. Provide a standing culture requisition.
  • Limited evidence: probiotics. Behavioural advice such as post-coital voiding and wiping direction isn’t supported by case-control data (Scholes 2000, women aged 18 to 30) but is harmless.
Hematuria and UTI
  • Microhematuria means 3 or more RBC/HPF on microscopic urinalysis. A dipstick alone isn’t enough.
  • If found during a UTI, treat and repeat microscopy about 6 weeks later. If it has cleared, no further work-up is needed for it.
  • If it persists, risk-stratify by age, sex, smoking, RBC count, persistence and history of visible hematuria (AUA/SUFU, amended 2025). Low risk: repeat urinalysis. Intermediate: cystoscopy and renal ultrasound. High: cystoscopy and CT urography.
  • Visible hematuria without infection carries a high enough cancer risk to warrant cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging.
Pharmacist prescribing for UTI in Ontario

Since 2023, Ontario pharmacists can assess and prescribe for uncomplicated cystitis (O. Reg. 256/24). Funding covers up to 3 UTI assessments per person in 365 days. Under the Ontario College of Pharmacists algorithm, pharmacists refer men, pregnant patients, and patients with upper-tract or systemic features, gross hematuria or confusion, immunocompromise, urinary tract abnormalities (catheter, stones, neurogenic bladder, renal dysfunction), relapse within 4 weeks, or recurrent UTI (2 in 6 months or 3 in 12 months). The algorithm doesn’t recommend cultures for acute uncomplicated cystitis, so patients with recurrent UTI may arrive without culture documentation.
