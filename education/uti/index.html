The Core Problem

Why Proper Diagnosis Matters

A critical gap in UTI care: patients are being referred for cystoscopy before a culture-proven UTI has ever been established. This causes unnecessary procedures and delays appropriate treatment.

🚨
Critical Clinical Issue

Patients should NOT be referred for cystoscopy based on symptoms alone. A urine culture must be obtained during a symptomatic episode before any further workup is initiated. Cystoscopy is only indicated for microhematuria or gross hematuria occurring outside of symptomatic UTI episodes.

🔬 Step 1: Culture First

Obtain a midstream urine culture when the patient is actively symptomatic. Empiric treatment without culture does not establish a diagnosis of UTI. Pyuria alone is insufficient.

💊 Step 2: Treat Appropriately

First-line antibiotics per guidelines: Nitrofurantoin, Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole, or Fosfomycin for uncomplicated UTI. Tailor based on local resistance patterns and culture sensitivities.

🔄 Step 3: Prevent Recurrence

After ≥2 culture-proven UTIs in 6 months or ≥3 in a year, consider prophylaxis strategies — behavioral, non-antibiotic, or antibiotic — based on evidence and patient preference.

🏥 Step 4: When to Refer

Referral to urology is indicated for hematuria (without active infection), recurrent UTI unresponsive to management, structural abnormalities, or complicated/atypical presentations.

Quick Reference

Diagnosing a UTI: The Basics

Symptoms That Suggest UTI

🔥 Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (Cystitis)

  • Dysuria (pain or burning with urination)
  • Urinary frequency (urinating more often than usual)
  • Urgency (sudden strong urge to urinate)
  • Suprapubic discomfort or pressure
  • Cloudy, dark, or foul-smelling urine
  • Hematuria (blood in urine) — see hematuria note below

🌡️ Upper Tract Symptoms (Pyelonephritis) — Seek Care Promptly

  • Fever ≥38°C (100.4°F)
  • Flank pain or back pain (costovertebral angle tenderness)
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Rigors (shaking chills)
  • Feeling systemically unwell

What Is NOT Sufficient to Diagnose UTI

⚠️
Dipstick alone is NOT enough

A positive urine dipstick for leukocyte esterase or nitrites supports UTI but cannot confirm it. Culture is required for diagnosis — especially before specialist referral or antibiotic prophylaxis.

⚠️
Asymptomatic Bacteriuria ≠ UTI

A positive urine culture without symptoms (asymptomatic bacteriuria, or ASB) should NOT be treated with antibiotics in most patients — this includes the elderly, diabetic patients, and women (exception: pregnancy and pre-urological procedures). Treating ASB drives antibiotic resistance and does not improve outcomes.

🔬
Hematuria Outside Infection

If blood is found in urine when the patient is NOT having a symptomatic UTI episode, this requires urology evaluation. Do not assume it is "just a UTI."

Diagnosing a UTI in Women

🔬 The Urine Culture: Your Most Important Test

A urine culture should be collected while you have symptoms. This is the only way to confirm a UTI, identify the bacteria causing it, and ensure the right antibiotic is chosen. Without a culture, treatment is guesswork.

How to collect a proper midstream urine sample:
  1. Wash hands thoroughly
  2. Use a clean-catch technique: wipe front-to-back with provided wipes
  3. Begin urinating into the toilet, then catch the middle portion of the stream in the cup
  4. Bring to lab or physician's office within 2 hours (or refrigerate up to 24 hours)

Clinical Diagnosis Criteria

📋 Uncomplicated Cystitis

Probable diagnosis when a healthy, non-pregnant, premenopausal woman has ≥2 of:

  • Dysuria
  • Frequency / urgency
  • Suprapubic pain
  • Hematuria

With no fever, no flank pain, no vaginal discharge/irritation suggesting alternative diagnosis

Culture Threshold

✅ Positive Culture Definition

  • ≥10³ CFU/mL in symptomatic women (IDSA)
  • ≥10⁵ CFU/mL historically used — now recognized as too high
  • Single organism typically expected
  • Common pathogens: E. coli (75–90%), Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Klebsiella, Proteus
ℹ️
Dipstick Interpretation

Positive leukocyte esterase + nitrites: ~85% sensitivity, ~70% specificity. Not sufficient alone for diagnosis or to guide antibiotic choice.

🚨
Do NOT Treat These Without Symptoms
  • Positive dipstick with no symptoms = do NOT treat
  • Positive culture with no symptoms (ASB) = do NOT treat (except pregnancy or pre-op urological procedure)
  • Cloudy or smelly urine alone = do NOT treat

Treatment: Uncomplicated Cystitis in Women

Before prescribing: Obtain a urine culture. If treating empirically, always review the culture result when available and adjust if needed (antibiotic stewardship).

💧 Phenazopyridine (Pyridium®)

Urinary analgesic — reduces dysuria and urgency. Not an antibiotic. Take with food. Turns urine orange — do not be alarmed. Use maximum 2 days alongside antibiotics. Available OTC in most countries.

Evidence: Symptom relief only — does not treat infection.

🔥 Ibuprofen / NSAIDs

For mild uncomplicated cystitis, ibuprofen alone for symptom relief has been studied (SINUT trial, ALTAR trial). Approximately 70% resolve without antibiotics. However, current guidelines still recommend antibiotics — discuss with your physician if you wish to trial this approach. Level 2

💊 Methenamine Hippurate

Urinary antiseptic — converts to formaldehyde in acidic urine. Used for prophylaxis, not acute treatment. Works best with acidic urine. Not appropriate if structural urinary tract abnormality or renal insufficiency. Level 1B

Recurrent UTI defined as: ≥2 culture-proven episodes in 6 months OR ≥3 in 12 months (IDSA/EAU). Each acute episode should still be treated with a full antibiotic course based on culture.

📋 Patient Self-Start Therapy

For women with recurrent, well-documented UTIs, patient-initiated therapy (self-start antibiotics kept at home) is guideline-endorsed. Requires:

  • Prior culture-proven UTIs with consistent presentation
  • Education on when to use (symptoms consistent with prior episodes)
  • Culture collection BEFORE starting antibiotic
  • Follow-up within 48–72 hours or sooner if no improvement

IDSA Level 1B

Preventing UTIs in Women

Mechanism: Proanthocyanidins (PACs) from cranberry inhibit P-fimbriated E. coli adherence to uroepithelium. Does NOT acidify urine sufficiently to kill bacteria.

✅ Evidence Summary

Cochrane 2023 meta-analysis (50 RCTs): cranberry products reduce UTI incidence in women with recurrent UTIs. NNT approximately 8–10. Modest but real benefit.

Level 1B — Multiple RCTs

📏 Effective Products & Doses

  • Capsules/tablets: ≥36 mg PAC daily (most consistent evidence)
  • Juice: 240–300 mL unsweetened twice daily — high caloric load, limited compliance
  • Brand examples: Ellura® (36 mg PAC), Utiva®, CranActin®
  • Not all cranberry products have equivalent PAC content — check label

⚠️ Who May NOT Benefit

  • Postmenopausal women (less clear benefit)
  • Those on warfarin — monitor INR
  • Ineffective for catheter-associated UTI
  • Does not replace antibiotics for acute infection
Mechanism: A naturally occurring sugar that competitively inhibits E. coli type-1 fimbrial adhesion to uroepithelium. Excreted unchanged in urine.

📊 Evidence

Several RCTs including Kranjčec 2014 (n=308) showed D-mannose 2g daily significantly reduced recurrent UTI compared to placebo (RR ~0.5). Comparable to nitrofurantoin prophylaxis in some studies.

Level 2 — RCTs with limitations

💊 Dosing

  • Prophylaxis: 2 g once daily in water
  • Acute use: 2 g every 2–3 hours × 3 days (limited data)
  • Generally well tolerated — loose stools at high doses
  • Caution in diabetes (glucose absorption)
Mechanism: Estrogen deficiency post-menopause leads to vaginal/urethral atrophy, loss of lactobacilli, elevated vaginal pH, and increased colonization with uropathogens. Vaginal estrogen reverses these changes.

✅ Strong Recommendation

Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses confirm vaginal estrogen reduces UTI frequency by 50–75% in postmenopausal women. IDSA and EAU both recommend as first-line prevention in this population.

Level 1A

💊 Available Formulations

  • Vaginal cream: Premarin®, Estrace® — apply 3× weekly
  • Vaginal ring: Estring® — insert every 90 days
  • Vaginal tablet/insert: Vagifem®, Imvexxy® — twice weekly
  • Ospemifene (oral SERM): Alternative if vaginal administration not tolerated

Systemic absorption is minimal with vaginal preparations — generally safe in most women (discuss with physician if breast cancer history)

⚠️
Requires physician prescribing — not OTC

Prophylactic antibiotics should be used only after culture-proven recurrent UTI. Cultures must be obtained before initiating any prophylaxis.

🔬 Evidence for Lactobacillus

Vaginal and oral Lactobacillus rhamnosus and L. reuteri strains show modest benefit in small RCTs. Cochrane review (2015): insufficient evidence to recommend as sole preventive strategy, but low risk and possible benefit as adjunct.

Level 2

💊 Products Studied

  • Oral: L. rhamnosus GR-1 + L. reuteri RC-14 (RepHresh Pro-B®)
  • Vaginal suppositories: L. crispatus studied in trials
  • After antibiotic course: recolonize with vaginal lactobacilli
  • Avoid if immunocompromised

💉 Uromune® (MV-140)

Sublingual polyvalent bacterial vaccine containing inactivated E. coli, K. pneumoniae, E. faecalis, P. vulgaris. Sprayed under tongue daily × 3 months. RCTs show 60–80% reduction in UTI frequency. Not yet licensed in all countries — available in Europe, UK, some Canadian centres.

Level 1B — multiple RCTs

💊 OM-89 (Uro-Vaxom®)

Oral capsule of E. coli extracts — immunostimulant. One capsule daily × 3 months, then 10 days per month × 3 months. Multiple RCTs show ~40% reduction in recurrence. Available in Europe and Canada.

Level 1B

🔬 Strovac® / Solco-Urovac®

Injectable polyvalent vaccine. Given as a series of IM injections. European data shows efficacy. Not widely available in North America currently.

Level 2

ℹ️
Evidence Note

Many behavioral recommendations are based on observational data and expert consensus rather than RCTs. Evidence level is lower but these are low-risk interventions.

💧 Hydration

Increase daily fluid intake to ≥1.5–2L/day. Hooton et al. 2018 (RCT): increasing water intake by 1.5L/day reduced UTI frequency by 50% in women with recurrent UTIs who drank <1.5L/day baseline. 1B

🚽 Voiding Habits

  • Void after sexual intercourse (within 30 minutes)
  • Do not delay urination when urge felt
  • Complete bladder emptying (double voiding if needed)

Expert Consensus

🧼 Hygiene

  • Wipe front-to-back after toileting
  • Avoid harsh soaps, douching, scented products in genital area
  • Cotton underwear preferred
  • Change out of wet bathing suits promptly

Expert Consensus

🛁 Products to Avoid

  • Spermicidal contraceptives — alter vaginal flora, major risk factor
  • Diaphragms (spermicide-coated)
  • Feminine hygiene sprays
  • Prolonged bubble baths

1B

🩲 Contraception Choices

  • Avoid spermicide — most important factor
  • Oral contraceptives, condoms without spermicide, IUDs: not associated with increased UTI risk
  • Condoms may provide minor protective effect against STI-related urethritis

🍵 Other Supplements (Limited Evidence)

  • Vitamin C: Theoretical acidification of urine — insufficient RCT evidence
  • Hibiscus extract: Small studies, insufficient evidence
  • Garlic/allicin: In vitro anti-adhesion, no human RCTs
  • Berberine: Some antimicrobial activity — insufficient clinical evidence

Level 3 — insufficient evidence

🔬 GAG Layer Replenishment (Hyaluronic Acid / Chondroitin Sulphate)

The glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer of the bladder is a protective barrier. Intravesical instillations of hyaluronic acid ± chondroitin sulphate (e.g., iAluRil®, IALURIL Prefill®) may help restore this barrier. Several RCTs show reduction in recurrent UTIs in refractory cases. Administered by a physician/nurse via catheter instillation monthly for 4–6 months.

Level 2 — EAU guideline mention

Special Situations

🚨
ASB Must Be Treated in Pregnancy

Unlike all other populations, asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy MUST be screened for and treated. It increases risk of pyelonephritis (25–30%), preterm birth, and low birthweight. Screen at first prenatal visit. Repeat cultures after treatment.

🔍 Why More Vulnerable

  • Estrogen deficiency → urogenital atrophy
  • Loss of protective Lactobacillus flora
  • Elevated vaginal pH → colonization with uropathogens
  • Incomplete bladder emptying (prolapse, detrusor dysfunction)
  • Post-void residual increases

💊 Prevention Priority: Vaginal Estrogen

Strongest evidence for this population. 1A

  • Intravaginal estradiol cream/tablet/ring
  • Restore flora, lower pH, thicken urothelium
  • Discuss with your doctor — minimal systemic absorption
  • Consider Ospemifene (oral SERM) if unable to use vaginal therapy

📋 Other Considerations

  • Pelvic floor physiotherapy for prolapse/incomplete emptying
  • Treat constipation (fecal colonization risk)
  • Ensure adequate hydration
  • Urine culture essential — polymicrobial colonization more common
  • Longer treatment courses may be needed (7 days nitrofurantoin)

📋 What Makes a UTI "Complicated"?

  • Structural: urinary tract abnormality, stones, obstruction
  • Functional: catheter, neurogenic bladder, post-void residual
  • Host factors: immunosuppression, diabetes (not uncomplicated per EAU if otherwise healthy), pregnancy, renal transplant
  • Organism: unusual pathogens, ESBL, MDR organisms

Management: Culture always required. Longer treatment (7–14 days). Urology input if structural cause identified. Address underlying cause.

🧪 Catheter-Associated UTI (CAUTI)

  • CAUTI = ≥10³ CFU/mL + symptoms in catheterized patient
  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria in catheterized patients = do NOT treat
  • Remove/change catheter before starting antibiotics when possible
  • Treatment duration: 7 days if prompt response, 10–14 days if delayed
  • Do not use prophylactic antibiotics for chronic catheter use

⚠️ If You Are Penicillin-Allergic

Cross-reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins is very low (<2%). A reported "penicillin allergy" does not mean you cannot have cephalosporins. Discuss allergy history with your physician. Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin are safe alternatives independent of penicillin allergy.

🦠 ESBL-Producing Organisms

Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) organisms are resistant to most penicillins and cephalosporins. If you have recurrent UTIs that don't respond to treatment, ESBL should be suspected. Options: nitrofurantoin (if susceptible), fosfomycin, or IV ertapenem for severe cases. Always culture-directed.

When Should Women Be Referred to a Urologist?

🔴
The Cystoscopy Question — Most Important Message

Cystoscopy is NOT indicated for recurrent uncomplicated UTIs alone. The ONLY indications for cystoscopy are: (1) microhematuria outside active infection, (2) gross hematuria without active infection, (3) suspected bladder pathology (mass, fistula), or (4) refractory symptoms not responding to adequate UTI management. A culture-proven UTI must be established before any specialist workup for recurrent UTI.

Why Male UTIs Are Different

⚠️
All UTIs in Men Are "Complicated" by Definition

Unlike women, a UTI in a male patient implies a functional or structural abnormality until proven otherwise. The prostate, longer urethra, and anatomic differences make ascending infection less common — when it occurs, an underlying cause must be sought. A urine culture is mandatory for all male UTIs.

🔍 Common Underlying Causes

  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) — incomplete bladder emptying
  • Prostatitis (acute or chronic)
  • Urethral stricture
  • Bladder dysfunction / neurogenic bladder
  • Urinary tract stones or obstruction
  • Indwelling catheter or recent instrumentation
  • Renal transplant
  • Immunosuppression / diabetes
  • Foreskin (uncircumcised men have higher colonization rates)
  • MSM (men who have sex with men) — rectal flora exposure

🔬 Culture is Mandatory

A midstream urine culture is essential for every male UTI. Do not treat empirically long-term without culture guidance. Atypical organisms and resistant bacteria are more common in men.

Sample collection:
  1. Retract foreskin (if present) and clean glans with provided wipe
  2. Begin urinating, then catch midstream into cup
  3. The initial stream can help clear urethral commensals

Types of UTI in Men

🔍 Symptoms

  • Dysuria, frequency, urgency
  • Suprapubic discomfort
  • Cloudy or blood-tinged urine
  • No fever typically (if fever → pyelonephritis or prostatitis)

📋 Workup Required

  • Urine culture (mandatory)
  • Prostate exam (digital rectal exam) by physician
  • Post-void residual (bladder ultrasound)
  • STI screening if clinically indicated (gonorrhea/chlamydia)
  • First UTI in adult male → renal ultrasound
🚨
Acute prostatitis is a medical emergency if severe

Urinary retention, high fever, sepsis — go to emergency immediately. Prostatic abscess is a life-threatening complication.

🔥 Symptoms

  • Fever, chills, rigors
  • Perineal/pelvic pain
  • Urinary symptoms (frequency, urgency, dysuria)
  • Difficulty urinating or urinary retention
  • Exquisitely tender prostate on DRE — gentle exam only
  • Bacteremia/sepsis possible

💊 Treatment

  • Blood cultures + urine culture before antibiotics
  • IV antibiotics if severe (ceftriaxone ± gentamicin)
  • Fluoroquinolones have excellent prostate penetration — first-line if mild and susceptible
  • Duration: 4–6 weeks (prevents chronic prostatitis/abscess)
  • Suprapubic catheter if urinary retention (not urethral)

📋 Diagnosis (NIH Classification Category II)

Recurrent UTIs caused by the same organism, originating from the prostate. Requires Meares-Stamey 4-glass test or simplified 2-glass test (pre and post prostatic massage cultures) to confirm prostatic source.

  • VB1: voided bladder (first 10 mL) — urethral sample
  • VB2: midstream — bladder sample
  • EPS: expressed prostatic secretion after DRE
  • VB3: voided bladder post-massage
  • 10× higher count in EPS/VB3 than VB1/VB2 = prostate source

💊 Treatment

  • Fluoroquinolones: Ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID or Levofloxacin 500 mg daily
  • Duration: 4–6 weeks minimum
  • TMP-SMX 160/800 mg BID × 1–3 months if fluoroquinolone resistant
  • Culture-directed always
  • Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin) — adjunct to improve urine flow

1B — EAU Guidelines

🔄 Recurrence

Up to 30% recurrence after treatment. If recurrent:

  • Repeat 4-glass test to confirm prostatic source
  • Longer treatment course
  • Urology referral
  • Consider low-dose suppressive antibiotics (prophylaxis) if recurrent, culture-proven
ℹ️
CPPS is NOT a bacterial infection

Chronic pelvic pain for ≥3 months without proven bacterial infection (negative cultures). Antibiotics beyond an initial trial are NOT indicated. Often mistakenly treated with repeated antibiotics. Urology referral is appropriate.

💊 Management of CPPS (Urology Referral Indicated)

  • Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin) — reduce outlet resistance
  • Anti-inflammatory agents (NSAIDs)
  • Pelvic floor physical therapy
  • Psychological support / CBT
  • Quercetin (500 mg BID) — small RCTs show benefit in NIH CPSI scores
  • Pregabalin/gabapentin for centralized pain
  • Single antibiotic trial only (if never tried) — do not repeat if negative cultures

Pyelonephritis in a male requires urgent workup — structural abnormality must be excluded. CT scan of abdomen/pelvis with contrast (or renal ultrasound as initial imaging). Blood cultures mandatory. Urology consultation for any complicated pyelonephritis or failure to improve within 48–72h of appropriate antibiotics.

  • Treatment similar to complicated pyelonephritis: fluoroquinolones or IV beta-lactams
  • Duration: 10–14 days
  • Follow-up cultures required after treatment

Preventing UTIs in Men

ℹ️
Prevention requires treating the underlying cause

In men, UTI prevention centers on identifying and correcting the predisposing anatomical or functional abnormality. Behavioral and supplement-based prevention plays a secondary role compared to women.

🔧 Treat Underlying Obstruction

  • BPH: medical (alpha-blockers, 5-alpha reductase inhibitors) or surgical (TURP, HoLEP) treatment
  • Urethral stricture: dilation or urethroplasty
  • Bladder stones: lithotripsy or surgical removal
  • Post-void residual >300 mL: clean intermittent catheterization (CIC)

💧 Hydration & Voiding

  • Adequate fluid intake (1.5–2 L/day)
  • Complete bladder emptying — double voiding if BPH
  • Timed voiding to minimize post-void residual
  • Limit caffeine and alcohol (bladder irritants)

Expert Consensus

🍒 Cranberry (Limited Male Data)

Data specifically in men is sparse — most UTI prevention trials enrolled women. However, cranberry PAC products are low-risk and may have benefit in men with recurrent UTIs from E. coli. Reasonable to try alongside primary prevention measures.

Level 3 — limited male-specific data

💊 Antibiotic Prophylaxis in Men

Continuous low-dose prophylaxis can be considered for culture-proven recurrent UTIs after underlying causes are excluded/treated. Requires urology input. Options similar to women but culture-directed. Duration limited to 6–12 months.

Expert Consensus / EAU

🩺 Prostate Health

  • Treat BPH promptly — chronic obstruction is a UTI risk factor
  • PSA testing as per age/risk guidelines
  • Regular follow-up with physician if recurrent UTIs
  • STI testing in sexually active men with urethral symptoms

🧪 Catheter Care

  • Use smallest gauge catheter possible
  • Prefer intermittent over indwelling catheter when possible
  • Maintain closed drainage system
  • Catheter care per protocol — daily cleaning of urethral meatus
  • Do NOT use prophylactic antibiotics for chronic catheters

When Should Men Be Referred to a Urologist?

🔴
Most Male UTIs Warrant Urology Input

A first UTI in a man who does not have an obvious explanation (catheter, recent instrumentation) should generally be evaluated for structural abnormality. However, a cystoscopy should NOT be done before culture-proven UTI has been established and treated, and hematuria (if present) evaluated outside of the acute infection period.

  • Spermicide avoidance
  • Post-coital voiding

🔴 Weak / Insufficient Evidence

  • Garlic / allicin
  • Hibiscus / herbal teas
  • Homeopathy
  • Acupuncture (for UTI specifically)
  • Berberine

Decision Framework: Choosing a Prevention Strategy

1

Confirm the Diagnosis First

Prevention strategies should only be initiated after culture-proven recurrent UTI (≥2 in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months). Never initiate prophylaxis based on symptoms alone.

2

Address Modifiable Risk Factors

Behavioral measures first: adequate hydration, void post-coital, avoid spermicide, treat constipation. These are free and have supporting evidence.

3

Non-Antibiotic Prevention (Preferred First)

Try cranberry + D-mannose combination, vaginal estrogen (postmenopausal), methenamine hippurate, and/or immunostimulants (OM-89/Uromune) before antibiotic prophylaxis when possible.

4

Antibiotic Prophylaxis if Needed

If non-antibiotic measures fail or are insufficient, low-dose antibiotic prophylaxis is guideline-endorsed. Choose based on culture sensitivities. Plan for 6–12 month reassessment.

5

Urology Referral for Refractory Cases

If recurrent UTIs persist despite all the above, refer to urology. Exclude structural abnormality, consider intravesical therapies, or reassess diagnosis (interstitial cystitis, other).

Physician Reference

Family Physician Clinical Reference

Evidence-based management of UTIs in primary care — guidelines synthesis from IDSA, EAU, CUA, and NICE for day-to-day clinical decisions.

⚕ The Critical Message for Family Physicians

🔬 Culture Before Everything

Do not refer for cystoscopy without a culture-proven UTI. Pyuria alone, dipstick positivity, or recurrent symptoms without positive cultures are NOT sufficient to establish recurrent UTI as a diagnosis or to trigger specialist referral.

🚫 Cystoscopy Criteria

Cystoscopy is indicated ONLY for: (1) microscopic hematuria outside active UTI, (2) gross hematuria, (3) suspected bladder pathology (mass, fistula), (4) refractory symptoms despite proven and adequately treated recurrent UTI after all primary care options exhausted.

⚠️ Do Not Treat ASB

Asymptomatic bacteriuria should not be treated in non-pregnant, pre-procedure patients. This includes elderly patients in long-term care, diabetics, and catheterized patients. Treating ASB drives resistance without benefit.

🦠 Antibiotic Stewardship

Reserve fluoroquinolones for complicated UTI, pyelonephritis, and prostatitis. First-line for uncomplicated female cystitis: nitrofurantoin, TMP-SMX, fosfomycin. Always review and document antibiogram results.

Diagnostic Algorithm — UTI in Primary Care

1

Assess Symptoms

Dysuria + frequency/urgency ± suprapubic pain: probable cystitis. Fever + flank pain: pyelonephritis. Vaginal discharge/irritation: consider vulvovaginitis, STI. Urethral discharge in men: gonorrhea/chlamydia first.

2

Urine Culture — Always Collect Before Starting Antibiotics

Midstream clean-catch. For women with classic symptoms of uncomplicated cystitis, empiric treatment acceptable — but culture should still be sent to confirm organism and sensitivities. Culture essential in: men, pregnant women, elderly, complicated UTI, recurrent UTI, treatment failure.

3

Classify the UTI

Uncomplicated: Healthy, non-pregnant, premenopausal woman with cystitis symptoms, no structural/functional abnormality.
Complicated: Male, pregnant, elderly, diabetic with complications, structural abnormality, catheter, immunosuppression, resistant organism, pyelonephritis, prostatitis.

4

Treatment Decision

Uncomplicated cystitis: empiric nitrofurantoin or TMP-SMX, adjust based on culture. Complicated/pyelonephritis: culture-directed, longer course, consider imaging. Always document culture result and whether treatment was adjusted.

5

Follow-Up Culture (Test of Cure)

Test of cure culture (5–7 days post-treatment) required for: pregnant women (all UTIs), complicated UTIs, pyelonephritis, male UTIs, treatment failure. Not required routinely for uncomplicated cystitis if symptoms resolved.

Managing Recurrent UTI in Primary Care

📋
Documentation Checklist Before Referral or Prophylaxis
  • ≥2 positive urine cultures in 6 months OR ≥3 in 12 months — documented in chart
  • Each episode treated with culture-directed therapy
  • Urinalysis and culture during at least one symptomatic episode
  • Underlying causes excluded (pregnancy, structural, STI, ASB vs. true UTI)
  • In women: vaginal estrogen offered if postmenopausal
  • Behavioral counseling completed

Non-Antibiotic Strategies to Offer

  • Vaginal estrogen 1A — postmenopausal
  • Cranberry PAC ≥36 mg/day 1B
  • D-Mannose 2g/day 2
  • Methenamine hippurate 1g BID 1B
  • Increased hydration (+1.5 L/day) 1B
  • OM-89/Uro-Vaxom 1B
  • Spermicide elimination 1B
  • Post-coital voiding Consensus

When Non-Antibiotic Fails

If ≥2–3 recurrences despite non-antibiotic measures, initiate antibiotic prophylaxis:

  • Discuss with patient: benefit vs. risk of resistance
  • Choose antibiotic based on most recent culture sensitivities
  • Nitrofurantoin or TMP preferred (low collateral)
  • Reassess at 6 months: culture any breakthrough UTI
  • Document indication and planned duration in chart
  • Consider urology referral if prophylaxis fails

🚨

  • Non-pregnant premenopausal women 1A
  • Postmenopausal women 1A
  • Older adults in the community or long-term care 1A
  • Persons with diabetes 1B
  • Persons with spinal cord injury 1B
  • Catheterized patients (short or long-term) 1A

Hematuria Evaluation Algorithm — AUA/CUA Guidelines

🔬
The Key Rule: Evaluate Hematuria Outside the Infection Window

Hematuria found only during a symptomatic UTI episode does NOT automatically require cystoscopy. Repeat urinalysis 4–6 weeks after successful treatment. If hematuria persists → initiate hematuria workup per guidelines.

1

Gross Hematuria — Any Episode

Any visible blood in urine (not during menses, not during UTI treatment) requires cystoscopy and upper tract imaging (CT urogram). Refer urgently to urology. Risk stratify for bladder cancer.

2

Microhematuria Found During UTI — Wait and Repeat

≥3 RBC/HPF on a single urinalysis during active UTI: Treat the UTI. Repeat urinalysis 4–6 weeks after treatment. If hematuria resolves → no further workup. If hematuria persists → proceed to step 3.

3

Confirmed Microhematuria Outside UTI

Persisting ≥3 RBC/HPF on two separate urinalyses, without active infection, without menstruation, without recent vigorous exercise, strenuous activity or catheterization artifact → formal hematuria workup: CT urogram + cystoscopy. Refer to urology.

4

Risk Stratification (AUA 2020)

Low risk: Women <50, no smoking, no chemical exposure, 3–10 RBC/HPF — renal ultrasound + cystoscopy (or surveillance acceptable in low-risk).
Intermediate/High risk: Age >50, smoking history, chemical exposure, prior gross hematuria, >10 RBC/HPF — CT urogram + cystoscopy mandatory.

Referral Decision Guide

When to Refer to Urology

Evidence-based referral criteria — preventing unnecessary cystoscopies while ensuring timely specialist care when truly indicated.

🚨
THE FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLE

A cystoscopy should NEVER be ordered for recurrent UTI symptoms alone without: (1) at least one positive urine culture during a symptomatic episode, AND (2) completion of appropriate primary care management including antibiotic treatment and prevention strategies. The only absolute indication for cystoscopy is hematuria (micro or gross) occurring outside of an active symptomatic UTI episode.
