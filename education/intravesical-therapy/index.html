Non-Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer

Your Guide to Intravesical Therapy

A complete resource for patients receiving bladder instillation treatments — including BCG, Gemcitabine, and Gemcitabine/Docetaxel therapy. Know what to expect, how to prepare, and when to call your care team.

Explore My Treatment 🚨 Red Flag Symptoms
🚨
When to seek immediate care: If you develop a fever above 38.5°C (101.3°F), severe difficulty urinating, blood clots blocking urine flow, chest pain, confusion, or inability to get up from bed after treatment — go to the Emergency Department or call 911 immediately.
Understanding Your Treatment

What is Intravesical Therapy?

Intravesical therapy means placing a medication directly into the bladder through a thin tube (catheter). Because the medication stays inside the bladder, it targets cancer cells locally while minimizing effects on the rest of your body.

Why Is This Treatment Given?

Most bladder cancers that are treated this way are called Non-Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer (NMIBC) — meaning the cancer has not grown into the muscle wall of the bladder. These are often described as:

  • Ta — superficial tumors on the bladder lining
  • T1 — tumors that invade the tissue just beneath the lining
  • CIS (Carcinoma in Situ) — flat, high-grade cancer confined to the lining

After the tumor is removed by a urologist (called TURBT — Transurethral Resection of Bladder Tumor), intravesical therapy is given to reduce the risk of the cancer coming back (recurrence) and in some cases to prevent it from progressing to a more serious stage.

🔬 How Does It Work?

A thin, flexible catheter is passed through the urethra into your bladder. The medication is slowly instilled and held in the bladder for a specific amount of time (usually 1–2 hours). You then urinate it out. This is done as an outpatient procedure — no surgery, no general anaesthesia.

Key Fact: NMIBC has a high recurrence rate — up to 70% without adjuvant treatment. Intravesical therapy significantly reduces this risk and is endorsed by all major urological guidelines (AUA, EAU, NCCN).

The Procedure, Step by Step

  1. Arrive at the clinic

    Arrive as instructed. You may be asked to have a comfortably full or empty bladder depending on your protocol.

  2. Catheter placement

    A nurse or urologist gently passes a small catheter through your urethra into your bladder. This may cause brief discomfort or pressure but should not be painful.

  3. Medication instilled

    The treatment solution is slowly dripped into your bladder through the catheter. The catheter is then removed.

  4. Hold time

    You hold the medication in your bladder for 1–2 hours (per your specific protocol). You may go about calm, comfortable activity — sitting or resting is fine. The medication naturally contacts all areas of the bladder during normal bladder filling.

  5. Void and go home

    When time is up, you urinate out the medication. Special precautions apply for the next 6 hours (see below). Most people drive themselves home and resume normal activities.

Treatment Options

Immunotherapy · First-Line Standard of Care

BCG (Bacillus Calmette-Guérin) Therapy

BCG is a live, weakened bacterium originally developed as a tuberculosis vaccine. When placed in the bladder, it activates your immune system to seek out and destroy cancer cells.

💡 Why BCG?

BCG is the most effective intravesical therapy for high-risk NMIBC and is recommended by all major guidelines (AUA, EAU, NCCN). It reduces recurrence by approximately 32% and progression to muscle-invasive disease by roughly 27% compared to surgery alone. It is particularly important for CIS (carcinoma in situ) where it achieves complete response in up to 70–80% of patients.

📅 Induction (SWOG Protocol)

  • 6 weekly instillations
  • One instillation per week
  • Duration: 6 weeks
  • Each instillation held 2 hours
  • Begin 4–6 weeks after TURBT

📅 Maintenance (SWOG Protocol)

  • 3 weekly instillations at months 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36
  • Total: up to 3 years if tolerated
  • Maintenance is essential — it reduces recurrence significantly compared to induction alone
⚠️
Important: BCG should NOT be given if you have a urinary tract infection, visible blood in the urine (macroscopic hematuria), or immediately after a traumatic catheterization. Always tell your nurse if you have any burning or symptoms before the instillation.

Chemotherapy · Well-Tolerated Alternative

Gemcitabine (Single Agent)

Gemcitabine is a chemotherapy drug that works by interfering with DNA replication in cancer cells, preventing them from multiplying. It is generally better tolerated than BCG with fewer systemic side effects.

💡 Why Gemcitabine?

Gemcitabine is used as an alternative to BCG — particularly when BCG is unavailable, contraindicated, or not tolerated — and also for intermediate-risk NMIBC. It acts directly on cancer cells within the bladder lining and has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing recurrence in multiple randomized trials. It is also used as maintenance therapy to keep cancer from returning after induction.

📅 Induction

  • 6 weekly instillations
  • Gemcitabine 2,000 mg in 50–100 mL saline
  • Hold time: 1–2 hours
  • Begin 4–6 weeks after TURBT (or sooner for peri-operative single dose)

📅 Monthly Maintenance

  • One instillation per month
  • Typically for 12 months (clinic may vary)
  • Same dose: 2,000 mg in 50–100 mL saline
  • Hold time: 1–2 hours
Peri-operative single dose: A single instillation of Gemcitabine given within 24 hours after TURBT has strong evidence for reducing recurrence in low-to-intermediate risk tumors. If your urologist ordered this, it was given while you were still in hospital after your surgery.

Sequential Chemotherapy · BCG-Unresponsive Disease

Gemcitabine + Docetaxel (Sequential)

This is a two-drug regimen where Gemcitabine and Docetaxel are given sequentially during the same visit — each drug attacks cancer cells through a different mechanism, making them highly complementary.

💡 Why Gemcitabine + Docetaxel?

This combination was developed at the University of Iowa for patients with BCG-unresponsive or BCG-intolerant NMIBC — particularly those who would otherwise face removal of the bladder (radical cystectomy). Published data shows 12-month recurrence-free rates of ~54% and 5-year rates of ~25%, offering meaningful bladder preservation for patients who have failed BCG. It is now listed in NCCN guidelines for BCG-unresponsive NMIBC.

📅 Induction (6 Weeks)

  • Weeks 1–6: weekly instillations
  • Gemcitabine 1,000 mg in 50 mL saline — hold 90 minutes, then void
  • Docetaxel 37.5 mg in 50 mL saline — instilled immediately after voiding, held 90 minutes
  • Total clinic time: approx. 4 hours

📅 Maintenance (Monthly → Quarterly)

  • Months 3, 6, 9, 12: monthly instillations
  • Then quarterly through 24–36 months (if no recurrence)
  • Same sequential protocol each visit
  • Cystoscopy surveillance continues per protocol
⚠️
Hydration tip for Gem/Doce: Because you will hold two instillations, this is a longer visit. Void before arriving. Do not drink large amounts of fluid in the 2 hours before your appointment to ensure adequate dwell time. Drink normally after leaving.

Preparation & Recovery

Before, During & After Your Instillation

✅ Before Your Treatment

  1. Restrict fluids 2–4 hours before

    Avoid drinking large amounts of water or fluids for 2–4 hours before your appointment. This ensures the medication is not immediately diluted or forced out. A small amount of water to stay comfortable is fine.

  2. Do not take diuretics that morning

    If you take a "water pill" (such as furosemide/Lasix or hydrochlorothiazide), talk to your care team about timing. Taking it before treatment may make it difficult to hold the medication.

  3. Check for infection symptoms first

    If you have pain/burning with urination, cloudy or foul-smelling urine, or fever — call the clinic before coming. Treatment should not be given during an active UTI.

  4. No BCG if recently catheter traumatized

    (BCG only) If your last instillation or catheterization caused bleeding, contact the clinic. BCG should be delayed after a traumatic catheterization to prevent systemic absorption.

  5. Wear comfortable clothing

    You may be repositioning yourself during the hold period. Loose, comfortable clothes are recommended.

  6. Arrange your day

    Most patients drive themselves and return to work. However, plan for 2–4 hours in the clinic and some fatigue or bladder symptoms afterward. Consider scheduling on a day before a day off, especially for BCG.

🔁 After Your Treatment

  1. Bleach/disinfect the toilet (BCG only)

    BCG is a live organism. After voiding, add a cup (~250 mL) of undiluted household bleach to the toilet, wait 15–20 minutes before flushing. Do this for 6 hours after each BCG instillation. This prevents environmental contamination.

  2. Wash hands and genital area thoroughly

    After voiding your instillation, wash your hands and genitals with soap and water. This is especially important for BCG.

  3. Drink plenty of fluids

    Once you have voided your treatment, drink 6–8 glasses of water over the rest of the day. This flushes residual medication from the bladder and reduces irritation.

  4. Urinate frequently

    Do not hold your urine for long periods after the treatment period has ended. Empty your bladder regularly.

  5. Sexual activity after BCG

    Avoid sexual intercourse for 48 hours after BCG. If you do engage within that period, a condom must be used to protect your partner from BCG exposure. This applies to both male and female patients.

  6. Rest if needed

    Some fatigue and bladder discomfort are normal. Rest and take acetaminophen (Tylenol) as needed. Avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) if you have kidney concerns — ask your care team.

During the hold time: Simply go about normal, calm activity — sitting, reading, or resting. You do not need to roll or reposition yourself. The medication naturally contacts the bladder lining through normal movement and filling. Use the restroom as soon as your hold time is complete.
What to Expect

Side Effects: Common vs. Concerning

Side effects vary by treatment type. Most are manageable and temporary. Understanding what's normal helps you know when something needs attention.

✅ Common & Expected (All Treatments)
  • Urgency & frequency — need to urinate more often, sometimes urgently
  • Dysuria — burning or stinging when you urinate
  • Mild hematuria — light pink or slightly red urine, usually for 1–3 days
  • Bladder pressure or cramping — ache in the lower abdomen
  • Fatigue — feeling tired, especially 12–24 hours after BCG
  • Low-grade flu symptoms (BCG) — mild body aches, chills, low fever <38.5°C
  • These symptoms typically resolve within 48–72 hours
⚠️ Worth Monitoring (Contact Clinic Within 24–48 hrs)
  • Symptoms lasting more than 3 days after instillation
  • Worsening rather than improving urinary symptoms
  • Moderate hematuria — visibly red or pink urine persisting >48 hrs
  • Low-grade fever >24 hours — especially with BCG
  • New skin rash — especially with Gemcitabine
  • Difficulty starting to urinate or slow stream
  • Joint pain or swelling (BCG-related reactive arthritis)
  • Significant nausea or appetite loss
🚨 Red Flags — Contact Urology Team TODAY or Go to Emergency
  • Fever >38.5°C (101.3°F)
  • Rigors (severe, uncontrollable shaking/chills)
  • Inability to urinate (urinary retention)
  • Large blood clots in urine
  • Confusion or disorientation
  • Rapid heart rate with low blood pressure
  • Severe joint pain (BCG septic arthritis)
  • Shortness of breath or chest pain
  • Allergic reaction: hives, throat swelling
  • Granulomatous reactions in lung, liver (BCG)
  • Severe pelvic pain not relieved by pain medication
  • Continuous, heavy bright-red bleeding

🚨 Red Flag Symptoms — Act Immediately

These symptoms require you to call your Urology team right away or go directly to the Emergency Department. Do not wait until your next appointment. Do not take a "wait and see" approach.

Fever above 38.5°C (101.3°F)
Especially 2–8 hrs after BCG. May indicate BCG sepsis — a medical emergency.
Rigors or severe chills
Uncontrollable shaking with fever after BCG requires urgent evaluation.
Complete inability to urinate
Urinary retention after instillation — may need catheter drainage.
Blood clots blocking urine flow
Small blood clots are common; large clots causing obstruction are not.
Confusion or altered consciousness
May indicate BCG sepsis or severe systemic reaction — call 911.
Chest pain or shortness of breath
Could indicate pulmonary reaction to BCG or other serious complication.
Severe joint pain or swelling
BCG can cause reactive arthritis — requires prompt medical assessment.
Severe allergic reaction
Hives, lip/tongue swelling, throat tightening — call 911 immediately.
Heavy continuous bleeding
Bright red urine that doesn't clear or increase in volume — urgent care.
Signs of sepsis
High fever + fast heart rate + low blood pressure + confusion = 911 now.
ℹ️
BCG Sepsis specifically: This is rare but can be life-threatening. If you develop high fever (>38.5°C) within 24 hours of BCG, go to your nearest Emergency Department and tell them you received intravesical BCG. Treatment is with isoniazid (INH) and/or other anti-tuberculous medications plus steroids — your emergency physician will contact your urology team.
Self-Management

Home Remedies & Symptom Relief

These evidence-informed strategies can help you manage common symptoms between and after instillations. Always discuss new supplements or medications with your care team first.

💧

Stay Well Hydrated

After your treatment, drink 8–10 glasses of water daily. Good hydration flushes irritants from the bladder, reduces the concentration of residual medication, and decreases dysuria. Avoid alcohol and caffeine on treatment days as they irritate the bladder.

🌡️

Warm Compresses / Heat Pad

A warm (not hot) heating pad applied to the lower abdomen can relieve bladder cramping and pelvic discomfort. Use for 20 minutes at a time with a layer of cloth between the pad and skin. Do not use if you have decreased skin sensation.

💊

Urinary Analgesics (AZO / Phenazopyridine)

Over-the-counter phenazopyridine (brand name AZO, Uristat) can reduce burning and urgency. Take as directed on the package. Note: it turns urine bright orange — this is harmless. Use for no more than 2 days at a time. Do not use if you have kidney disease.

🍵

Soothing Fluids

Chamomile or peppermint herbal tea (caffeine-free) can be soothing. Diluted cranberry juice may provide some comfort (though it doesn't treat UTI in this context). Avoid citrus juices, alcohol, and carbonated drinks on treatment days as they worsen bladder irritation.

🛁

Sitz Baths

A warm sitz bath (sitting in a few inches of warm water in the tub) for 10–15 minutes can relieve urethral irritation, external burning, and pelvic discomfort, especially in women. Avoid scented bath products, bath bombs, or soap inside the bath water.

😴

Rest & Sleep

Schedule your instillations before a day off when possible — especially for BCG. Fatigue and bladder symptoms are worst 12–24 hours after treatment and usually improve by 48–72 hours. Listen to your body and rest when needed.

🚫

Bladder Irritant Avoidance

For 24–48 hours after treatment, avoid: caffeine, alcohol, spicy foods, artificial sweeteners, carbonated drinks. These can worsen urgency and burning. Some people find keeping a "bladder diary" helps identify their personal triggers.

🧘

Pelvic Floor Exercises

Kegel exercises can improve bladder control and reduce urgency over time. Ask your urology team for a referral to a pelvic floor physiotherapist — they can tailor a program specifically for you. This is especially helpful if urgency incontinence develops.

🌿

Acetaminophen for Fever/Discomfort

Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is the preferred pain reliever during intravesical therapy. Follow standard dosing. Avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) unless specifically approved by your urologist, as they can increase bleeding risk and affect kidney function.

🛡️

Petroleum Jelly (Gemcitabine)

For Gemcitabine treatments, applying a thin layer of petroleum jelly (Vaseline) to the urethral opening and surrounding skin before your appointment can help prevent skin and chemical irritation in the external genital area.

📱

Bladder Training

If urgency and frequency are significantly affecting your quality of life, bladder training — gradually increasing the time between bathroom visits — can be helpful. This is done under guidance of your care team or pelvic physiotherapist.

🧠

Mental Health & Support

A bladder cancer diagnosis and ongoing treatment can cause anxiety and emotional distress. This is normal. Cancer support groups (BCAN — Bladder Cancer Advocacy Network), counselling, and mindfulness practices are all beneficial. Ask your team for a referral to oncology social work if needed.

⚠️
Probiotics: Some patients ask about probiotics during treatment. There is no evidence they interfere with intravesical therapy. However, if you are on BCG — which is a live organism — discuss any supplements with your care team before starting them, as certain supplements may theoretically affect immune response.
Ongoing Monitoring

Surveillance & Follow-Up

Bladder cancer surveillance is ongoing and lifelong. Even after successful treatment, regular monitoring is essential because recurrence rates are significant.

What Is Cystoscopy?

A cystoscopy is a procedure where a thin, flexible camera (cystoscope) is passed through your urethra into your bladder. Your urologist looks directly at the bladder lining to check for any suspicious areas.

It is usually done in the office with local anaesthetic gel and takes about 5–10 minutes. There is some pressure or brief stinging but it should not be severely painful.

Tip: You may have mild burning when you urinate for 24 hours after a cystoscopy. Drink extra water. If you develop fever, chills, or heavy bleeding after cystoscopy, contact your care team.

About This Resource

This patient education guide was developed for urology patients receiving intravesical therapy for non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer.
