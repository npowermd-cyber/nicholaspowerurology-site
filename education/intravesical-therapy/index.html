Urology education

Bladder treatments: BCG and chemotherapy

For bladder cancer that has not grown into the bladder muscle: why medicine is put into your bladder, what each treatment day is like, side effects, warning signs, and a calendar of your treatments and check-ups.

The medicine stays in your bladder. It goes in through a thin, soft tube (a catheter) and you pass it out when you urinate. Very little reaches the rest of your body, so there is no hair loss and usually no nausea.
Your risk group sets the plan. What the lab found in the tumour that was removed puts your cancer in a low, intermediate or high risk group. This decides which treatment you have and for how long.
Most side effects settle in 1 to 2 days. Burning and needing to pass urine often are common. A few signs, such as a fever with shaking chills after BCG, need help right away.

Go to the nearest emergency department, or call 911, if you have:

  • a temperature of 38.5°C or higher, or shaking chills (shaking you can’t stop), especially in the days after BCG;
  • yellow skin or eyes after BCG;
  • you can’t pass urine, or only a few drops, and your bladder feels full;
  • heavy bleeding, or clots that block your urine;
  • trouble breathing, confusion, or you feel very unwell.

Tell them you are having treatment in your bladder for bladder cancer, which medicine (for example BCG), and the date of your last treatment. All warning signs

Part 1

Your cancer and your risk group

Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (often shortened to NMIBC) is cancer in the inner lining of the bladder, or the layer just under it, that has not grown into the bladder’s muscle wall. Most bladder cancers are found at this stage.

The tumour is removed in an operation done through the urethra (the tube you urinate through), called a TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour). There is no cut on the skin. The lab then looks at what was removed.

This kind of bladder cancer is rarely life-threatening, but it often comes back somewhere in the bladder lining, and some types can grow deeper into the muscle over time. The treatments on this page lower those chances. That is also why you will have regular camera checks of your bladder for years.

The words on your pathology report
  • Ta: a growth that sticks out from the lining, like a small piece of seaweed or a cauliflower, and has not grown under the lining.
  • T1: the cancer has grown into the layer just under the lining, but not into the muscle.
  • CIS (carcinoma in situ, also written Tis): flat, red patches of fast-growing cancer cells in the lining. It does not stick out, but it can spread through the lining and grow deeper if it is not treated.
  • Low grade or high grade: how abnormal the cells look under the microscope. High-grade cells are more likely to come back and to grow deeper.
  • Size and number: one tumour or several, and whether the biggest was more than 3 cm (about the width of a large grape).
  • Muscle present: means the sample was deep enough to check the muscle. If no muscle was in the sample, a second TURBT may be advised.

The three risk groups

Your risk group is a way of saying how likely the cancer is to come back or grow deeper. It is worked out each time a tumour is found, so it can change. Ask Dr. Power or your nurse which group you are in, then use it in the calendar below.

Low risk

One small (3 cm or less), low-grade Ta tumour, found for the first time.

  • Usual plan: one dose of chemotherapy in the bladder within 24 hours of the TURBT, then regular camera checks.
  • A course of weekly treatments is not usually needed.
  • It may come back, but the chance of it growing into the muscle is very small.

Intermediate risk

Low-grade cancer only: more than one low-grade tumour, a low-grade tumour bigger than 3 cm, or a low-grade tumour that came back within a year.

  • Usual plan: often a single dose after the TURBT, then for many people a 6-week course of weekly treatments. This may be chemotherapy (gemcitabine, gemcitabine–docetaxel or mitomycin) or BCG.
  • Some people go on to maintenance: repeat treatments spread out over the following months, to help keep the cancer from coming back. For intermediate risk this is usually for up to 1 year.
  • The main concern is the cancer coming back. Growing deeper is less common.

High risk

Any high-grade tumour (Ta or T1, of any size) or any CIS.

  • Usual plan: often a second TURBT within about 6 weeks, especially for T1, to make sure nothing was missed. Then BCG: 6 weekly treatments, then maintenance for up to 3 years. Gemcitabine–docetaxel is an alternative used in many centres.
  • Some features carry extra risk, such as unusual cell types (called subtypes), cancer seen in tiny blood or lymph vessels, or cancer in the prostate part of the urethra. With these, removing the bladder (cystectomy) may be discussed early.
  • The aim is to keep the cancer from growing into the muscle while keeping your bladder.

Risk groups follow the 2026 American Urological Association and Society of Urologic Oncology (AUA/SUO) guideline, in simplified form. Low-grade T1 cancer is uncommon; ask your team which group it puts you in. The European Association of Urology uses a scoring system with a fourth, very high risk group, where early bladder removal is often discussed. The groups on your report may differ slightly depending on which system your team uses.

Part 2

Your treatments

Putting medicine straight into the bladder is called intravesical therapy. Each treatment is called an instillation. A course usually starts with induction, one treatment a week for 6 weeks, and may continue with maintenance, repeat treatments spread out over months or years to keep the cancer from coming back. There are four main kinds of treatment. You may have more than one over time.

  1. Check in. A nurse asks how you have been and tests your urine for infection.
  2. Catheter. After numbing gel, a thin, soft tube is passed into your bladder. This is uncomfortable for a few seconds but should not be painful.
  3. Medicine in. The medicine runs in slowly. The tube is usually taken out right away.
  4. Hold it. You keep the medicine in your bladder for about 1 to 2 hours. You can sit, walk around, or go home.
  5. Pass it out. You urinate sitting down, then follow the precautions for the next few hours.

A single dose of chemotherapy after surgery

One treatment, given within 24 hours of your TURBT, usually while you are still in the recovery area.

Low and intermediate risk

Some cancer cells can float free during surgery and settle back on the bladder lining. A dose of chemotherapy soon after surgery kills many of them. In Canada this is usually gemcitabine; some hospitals use mitomycin. It is held for about an hour, then drained through a catheter or passed in the toilet. You may not remember having it, so ask if you aren’t sure.

How much it helps

In a large North American trial (SWOG S0337), people whose tumour looked low grade had either gemcitabine or salt water (saline) put in the bladder right after surgery. Over 4 years, the cancer came back in:

Salt water

47 out of 100

Gemcitabine

35 out of 100

So about 12 fewer people in every 100 had their cancer come back. Serious side effects were uncommon, about 2 to 3 in 100, and no more common than with salt water.

When it is not given

It is skipped if the bladder wall may have been punctured, if a lot of tissue was removed, or if there was heavy bleeding. It is less useful for high-risk cancer, where a full course of BCG or gemcitabine–docetaxel follows anyway.

BCG

A weakened germ that wakes up your immune system to attack cancer cells in the bladder lining.

High risk, and some intermediate risk

BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin) is a live but weakened bacterium, first made as a vaccine against tuberculosis (TB). In the bladder, it causes an immune reaction that clears cancer cells. It is not TB, and you cannot catch or spread TB from it. For high-risk cancer, BCG with maintenance has been the standard treatment for over 20 years.

Schedule
  • Starts about 2 to 6 weeks after your TURBT, once the bladder has healed.
  • Induction: one treatment a week for 6 weeks.
  • A cystoscopy (camera check) about 3 months after the first treatment.
  • Maintenance: if the bladder is clear, 3 weekly treatments at about 3, 6 and 12 months, then every 6 months: at 18, 24, 30 and 36 months. This is called the SWOG schedule.
  • How long: up to 3 years for high-risk cancer (as many as 27 treatments), and usually 1 year for intermediate risk.
  • If CIS or high-grade Ta cancer is still there at the 3-month check, a second 6-week course may be given, and the maintenance dates move later. If high-grade T1 cancer is still there after the first course, more BCG is unlikely to work, and removing the bladder is usually discussed.
  • Each BCG treatment is held for 2 hours.
How much it helps
  • BCG lowers the chance of high-risk cancer coming back and of it growing into the muscle, compared with surgery alone or with chemotherapy given the older way.
  • Maintenance matters. In the trial that set the schedule (SWOG 8507), people who had maintenance went about twice as long before their cancer came back as those who stopped after the first 6 weeks: a middle value of about 6 years compared with about 3.
  • Many people don’t finish every maintenance treatment. In that trial, only 16 in 100 had all of them, mostly because of side effects or the burden of visits. If side effects are hard, tell your team: the plan can often be adjusted.
Side effects
  • Most people have burning, needing to go often and urgently, and sometimes blood in the urine for 1 to 2 days after each treatment. These often get stronger after the third or fourth weekly treatment.
  • Flu-like feelings, tiredness and a mild temperature for a day or two are common. This is the immune system working.
  • Less often: joint pain, red or sore eyes, a swollen testicle, or a lasting cough. Call us if you have any of these.
  • BCG infection in the body is uncommon (about 1 in 50 to 1 in 250 people, by the British Association of Urological Surgeons’ estimate) but serious. Its main sign is a high fever or shaking chills. It needs treatment in hospital with TB medicines. See when to get help.
Newer combinations with BCG

Recent trials added an immunotherapy medicine to BCG for high-risk cancer that has not had BCG before: durvalumab, given by vein (the POTOMAC trial), and sasanlimab, given by injection under the skin (the CREST trial). Fewer people had their cancer come back, roughly 5 fewer in every 100 at 2 years, but it did not lower the chance of the cancer growing into the muscle, and there were more side effects. Durvalumab with BCG was approved in the United States in 2026. These combinations are not standard treatment in Ontario yet.

Who should not have BCG

BCG is not given, or is delayed, if you have a urine infection, visible blood in the urine, or a difficult catheter that caused bleeding that day. It is usually avoided if your immune system is weakened, for example by anti-rejection medicines after a transplant, high-dose steroids, some cancer treatments or untreated HIV, and during pregnancy or breastfeeding. Tell your team if you have ever had TB.

BCG shortages

BCG is made by only a few companies, and supply has been short at times around the world, including in Canada. When it is short, teams may give BCG to the people who need it most, use a lower dose, or use gemcitabine–docetaxel instead. If your plan changes for this reason, it is still a recognized treatment.

Gemcitabine–docetaxel (“Gem/Doce”)

Two chemotherapy medicines given one after the other at the same visit.

High and intermediate risk, and cancer that comes back after BCG

Gemcitabine goes in first and is held, then drained. Docetaxel goes in next and is held. The two drugs attack cancer cells in different ways. Many Canadian centres use Gem/Doce for high-risk cancer, especially when BCG is in short supply, when BCG isn’t suitable or wasn’t tolerated, and when cancer comes back after BCG.

Schedule
  • Induction: one treatment a week for 6 weeks, usually starting a few weeks after your TURBT.
  • A cystoscopy about 3 months after the first treatment.
  • Maintenance: if the bladder is clear, one treatment a month, starting about 3 months after the first treatment. For high-risk cancer this is usually for up to 2 years (as many as 24 monthly treatments, as in the BRIDGE trial). For intermediate risk, often 1 year.
  • Each drug is held for about 1 to 2 hours, so a visit can take 3 to 4 hours. Some clinics let you go home with the second drug in your bladder.
  • Some teams ask you to take sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) tablets the night before and the morning of treatment. Do this only if your team tells you to.
How much it helps
  • Studies from several centres, mainly the University of Iowa, found results for high-risk cancer that look at least as good as BCG, with fewer people having to stop because of side effects. These studies looked back at past patients, which can be misleading, so they are not proof.
  • A large trial that randomly assigned people to Gem/Doce or BCG (the BRIDGE trial, ECOG-ACRIN EA8212) has finished enrolling patients. Its results are not yet available. Until then, both are reasonable choices, and BCG remains the long-standing standard.
  • For cancer that comes back after BCG, the 2026 AUA/SUO guideline lists Gem/Doce as one of the recommended options for people who can’t have, or don’t want, bladder removal.
Side effects
  • Burning, needing to go often and urgently, and blood in the urine for a day or two are common.
  • Flu-like feelings are less common than with BCG, and there is no risk of BCG infection.
  • Rarely, an allergic reaction to docetaxel (hives, itching, swelling of the lips or face, trouble breathing) while it is in the bladder. Tell the nurse at once.
  • Effects on the rest of the body, such as low blood counts, are very rare because so little is absorbed.

Gemcitabine or mitomycin on its own

A course of one chemotherapy medicine.

Mostly intermediate risk

Usually one treatment a week for 6 weeks. Some people then have one a month for up to a year. Each treatment is held for 1 to 2 hours. Side effects are mostly burning and needing to go often for a day or so.

Mitomycin and your skin

Mitomycin can cause a rash where urine touches the skin, and sometimes on the palms of the hands. Wash your hands and genitals with soap and water after you urinate, and tell your team about any rash.

If the cancer comes back after BCG

There are several choices, and Dr. Power will go through them with you.

The options
  • More bladder treatment: another course of BCG, or Gem/Doce.
  • Bladder removal (radical cystectomy): the most reliable way to stop high-risk cancer from spreading, but a major operation that changes how you pass urine. See Preparing for bladder removal surgery.
  • Clinical trials: London Health Sciences Centre takes part in bladder cancer trials. Ask whether one fits you.
  • Newer medicines: for high-risk cancer with CIS that no longer responds to BCG, Health Canada has approved pembrolizumab (an immunotherapy given by vein, approved with conditions) and, since March 2026, nadofaragene firadenovec (a gene therapy put in the bladder). Others are approved only in the United States. In Ontario, availability and public funding are limited and change over time.

Part 3

On treatment day

A little preparation helps the medicine work and keeps the people around you safe. If your clinic gives you its own instruction sheet, follow it where it differs from this page.

Before

  • Limit fluids for about 4 hours before your appointment, so the medicine isn’t diluted and is easier to hold. Small sips to take pills are fine. Skip coffee, tea and alcohol.
  • Water pills (diuretics, such as furosemide): take them after the treatment, once you have passed the medicine, unless your doctor has told you otherwise.
  • Keep taking your other medicines, including blood thinners, unless you are told to stop. Bring your medicine list.
  • Plan to be at the clinic for 1 to 3 hours (3 to 4 hours for Gem/Doce). Many people book BCG before a quieter day.

Tell the nurse first if you have

  • burning, cloudy or smelly urine, or a fever: you may have a urine infection, and treatment may need to wait;
  • blood in your urine, or bleeding after your last catheter;
  • started antibiotics, or any medicine that weakens the immune system, such as steroid pills;
  • a chance you could be pregnant, or you are breastfeeding;
  • side effects from your last treatment that lasted more than 2 days.

While you hold it

  • Hold BCG for 2 hours, and chemotherapy for as long as your nurse says (usually 1 to 2 hours).
  • You can sit, walk or go home. You don’t need to roll from side to side; the medicine reaches the whole lining on its own.
  • Don’t drink much until you have passed it.
  • If the urge becomes too strong, pass it. Don’t put up with severe pain. Tell your nurse how long you held it.

For the next few hours: protecting yourself and others

The medicine you pass is still active, and BCG is a live germ. These steps keep it off your skin and away from other people.

After BCG

For 6 hours after each treatment
  • Sit down to urinate, men too, to avoid splashing.
  • Each time, pour about 2 cups (500 mL) of household bleach into the toilet, close the lid, wait 15 minutes, then flush.
  • Wash your hands and genitals with soap and water after each time.
  • Clean any splashes with diluted bleach (2 tablespoons in 1 cup of water). Wash clothes with urine on them separately.
  • Use your own toilet if you can. After 6 hours, no special toilet steps are needed.
Sex
  • No sex for 48 hours after each treatment.
  • Use a condom for 1 week after each treatment, for any kind of sex, because BCG can be in semen or vaginal fluid.

After chemotherapy

Gemcitabine, Gem/Doce or mitomycin

For 6 hours after each treatment
  • Sit down to urinate, close the lid, and flush twice.
  • Wash your hands and genitals with soap and water after each time. This matters most with mitomycin, which can irritate the skin.
  • Wipe up any splashes with soap and water, wearing gloves if you can.
  • If you leak urine, use pads, change them often, and put used pads in a sealed bag in the garbage.
Sex
  • Use a condom for 48 hours after each treatment, to keep the medicine away from your partner.

After either

  • Once the medicine is out, drink more than usual for the next day or two (about 2 to 3 litres a day) to flush your bladder, unless you have been told to limit fluids for heart or kidney problems.
  • Empty your bladder often. Don’t hold urine for long.
  • You can hug, share meals and be around children and pets as usual.
  • Most people are back to their normal routine the next day. You can usually drive yourself home.
Pregnancy and fertility
  • Don’t start a pregnancy during treatment. Use reliable birth control, whether you or your partner could become pregnant, and ask your team how long to wait after treatment ends.
  • Don’t breastfeed during treatment.
  • A pregnant person in your home should avoid touching your urine.

Hospitals vary a little in these steps, for example in whether they still ask for bleach after BCG and in how long to use condoms. The times above are common Canadian practice. Your clinic’s own sheet comes first.

Part 4

Side effects and when to get help

Most side effects stay in the bladder and settle within 1 to 2 days. Knowing what is normal helps you spot the few things that are not.

Common and expected

In the first 1 to 2 days after a treatment

  • Needing to pass urine often or in a hurry, sometimes with leaking.
  • Burning or stinging when you urinate.
  • Pink urine, small clots, or bits of tissue in the urine.
  • An ache low in your belly, or tiredness.
  • After BCG: flu-like aches, feeling chilly, and a temperature below 38.5°C, for up to 2 days.
  • After mitomycin: mild skin irritation where urine touched.
Get advice the same day

Call the clinic that gave your treatment or our office at 1-888-909-2786. If you can’t reach us, call Health811 (dial 811), any time.

  • Symptoms that last more than 2 days, or get worse instead of better.
  • Blood in the urine for more than 2 days.
  • Burning with cloudy or smelly urine (possible infection).
  • After BCG, a temperature below 38.5°C that lasts more than 2 days.
  • New pain or swelling in your joints; red, sore eyes; a cough that won’t go away; a painful or swollen testicle.
  • A new rash, or nausea or vomiting.
Go to emergency now

Or call 911

  • A temperature of 38.5°C or higher, or shaking chills, especially after BCG. This can be a BCG infection, which needs treatment quickly.
  • A high temperature with pain in your back or side (possible kidney infection).
  • After BCG, yellow skin or eyes, which can mean BCG is affecting the liver.
  • You can’t pass urine, or only a few drops, and your bladder feels full.
  • Heavy bleeding, or clots that block your urine.
  • Trouble breathing, chest pain, confusion, or feeling faint or very unwell.
  • Hives, or swelling of the lips, tongue or throat.

Chills: common or an emergency?

  • Common after BCG: feeling cold or chilly, goosebumps, wanting a blanket, along with aches, for a day or two. Take your temperature.
  • Emergency: shaking chills (also called rigors), where your whole body shakes or your teeth chatter and you can’t stop it, often with a high temperature. Go to emergency, even if your temperature is below 38.5°C or you have taken acetaminophen.

At the emergency department

Say: “I am having BCG in my bladder for bladder cancer. My last treatment was on (date).” (Or name your chemotherapy medicine.) A BCG infection is treated differently from other infections, with TB medicines, so this is important. Ask the emergency team to contact the urologist on call. Keep the wallet card with you to show them.

Easing the common side effects

  • Drink plenty once the medicine is out, mostly water. For a day or two, cut down on coffee, tea, alcohol, fizzy drinks and spicy food, which can make burning and urgency worse.
  • Acetaminophen (Tylenol) helps aches, flu-like feelings and a mild temperature. Check your temperature before you take it, because it can hide a fever that needs attention. Ask before using ibuprofen or naproxen if you take a blood thinner or have kidney problems.
  • A warm (not hot) heating pad on your lower belly, with a cloth between it and your skin, can ease cramping.
  • Phenazopyridine is a bladder pain reliever. Ask your pharmacist whether it is right for you. It turns urine orange, and it is only for a day or two. Avoid it if you have kidney disease.
  • Plan rest for the evening of treatment and the next day, especially with BCG.
  • If the side effects are making it hard to keep going, tell your team. They may delay a treatment, adjust the dose, or prescribe medicine for bladder spasms. Please don’t just skip treatments.

What to tell your other doctors

Make sure your family doctor, dentist, pharmacist and any surgeon know you have had bladder cancer treatment, and which medicine.

  • If you have had BCG, tell them for the rest of your life. Very rarely, BCG germs can cause an infection months or even years later, for example in a joint, the spine, a blood vessel or an artificial joint. Knowing about the BCG helps doctors find and treat it.
  • BCG can make a TB skin test or TB blood test positive. Tell whoever is testing you.
  • Tell your team before any vaccine, any new medicine that weakens the immune system, or any antibiotic during your course, as some can affect BCG.
  • Planning surgery elsewhere, such as a joint replacement? Let both teams know about your bladder treatment schedule.

Part 5

Check-ups

Because this kind of cancer often comes back, you will have regular camera checks of your bladder for years, even when every check is clear. Finding a new tumour early, while it is small, keeps treatment simple.

Cystoscopy

A thin, flexible camera is passed into your bladder through the urethra after numbing gel. It takes about 5 minutes in the clinic, and you are awake. You may have mild burning for a day afterward. During BCG maintenance, the check is usually done just before each 3-week block.

Urine tests

A urine sample may be sent to look for cancer cells (urine cytology). This matters most for high-grade cancer and CIS, which can be hard to see with the camera.

Scans

The kidneys and the tubes that drain them (ureters) have the same lining as the bladder. With intermediate- or high-risk cancer, a CT scan of these (a CT urogram) is often done every 1 to 2 years.

How often, by risk group

While each check is clear. If you had only the single dose after surgery, count from the date of your surgery. If you are having a weekly course (BCG or chemotherapy), count from your first weekly treatment.

  • Low risk: first check at about 3 months, the next 6 to 9 months later, then once a year. After 5 clear years, you and your urologist may decide to stop or space them out.
  • Intermediate risk: every 3 to 6 months for the first 2 years, every 6 to 12 months in years 3 and 4, then once a year.
  • High risk: every 3 to 4 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months in years 3 and 4, then once a year, usually for many years, with urine cytology at each visit.

From the AUA/SUO guideline (2024 amendment; the 2026 amendment’s summary of changes lists no change to these intervals). The Canadian Urological Association and Alberta’s 2025 provincial guideline use very similar timing. If a tumour comes back, the clock restarts and your risk group is reviewed.

Between check-ups, call our office if you see blood in your urine, or have new burning or urgency that is not from a recent treatment.

Part 7

Questions people often ask

Does the treatment hurt?

Putting the catheter in feels uncomfortable for a few seconds; numbing gel helps. Holding the medicine feels like a full bladder. Burning when you pass urine afterward is common and usually settles within a day or two. If a catheter was difficult or painful, tell the nurse next time, so they can plan for it.

Can I work, drive and exercise?

Most people drive themselves home and go back to their usual routine the same or next day. After BCG, you may feel tired or flu-ish for a day or so; many people book treatments before a day off. Light activity is fine. Wait until any burning settles before hard exercise or a long bike ride.

Is this like the chemotherapy people get by vein?

No. The medicine stays in your bladder and very little gets into your blood, so you don’t get the hair loss, low blood counts or strong nausea that chemotherapy given by vein can cause. Side effects are mostly in the bladder.

What if I can’t hold it for the full time?

Pass it when you have to, and tell your nurse how long you managed. Holding it for part of the time still helps. Limiting fluids beforehand and emptying your bladder just before the treatment make holding easier, and it usually gets easier with practice.

What if a treatment is missed or delayed?

Call to rebook as soon as you can. One delayed treatment, for example because of a urine infection, is common and not a reason to worry. Don’t try to “catch up” by doubling up. Your team will reset the schedule. The later dates in your calendar will shift too.

Am I a risk to my family?

No, as long as you follow the toilet and hand-washing steps for 6 hours after each treatment, and the advice on sex. Outside those times, you can hug, share meals, and be around children, pets and people who are pregnant or have weak immune systems as usual. BCG is not TB and does not spread through the air.

How will I know if it is working?

Through your cystoscopy and urine tests, starting about 3 months after the first treatment. How strong your side effects are doesn’t tell you whether the treatment is working. Mild side effects do not mean it isn’t.

What if the cancer comes back?

A small low-grade tumour that comes back is common and is often treated simply, sometimes in the clinic. If high-grade cancer comes back despite treatment, Dr. Power will talk with you about the options, from another course of bladder treatment to a clinical trial or removing the bladder. A recurrence does not automatically mean losing your bladder. See If the cancer comes back after BCG.

Why is my plan different from someone else’s?

Plans depend on the risk group, on what was found at earlier check-ups, on your other health conditions and preferences, and sometimes on BCG supply. Two people with “bladder cancer” can have very different treatments, and both can be right.

Is there anything I can do myself?

Yes. If you smoke, stopping is the most helpful thing you can do: smoking is the main cause of bladder cancer and makes it more likely to come back. Free help is available from a quit coach at 1-866-366-3667, and your family doctor or pharmacist can help with medicines. Beyond that, keep your check-up appointments, drink enough fluids, and stay as active as you can.

Who to call

  • Emergency: go to the nearest emergency department or call 911 for the warning signs above.
  • Questions about your treatment or side effects: the clinic where you have your treatments, or Dr. Power’s office at 1-888-909-2786. Have your health card number and email address ready when you leave a message. The office is not for emergencies.
