Step 2 — The Full Spectrum of Prostate Cancer
Understanding that not all prostate cancers are the same is central to making an informed decision

Prostate cancer is not one disease — it encompasses a wide range of conditions from harmless microscopic changes that will never affect a man's life, to aggressive cancers that can be life-threatening. This is the most important concept in this entire guide.

1 in 8
Men diagnosed with prostate cancer in their lifetime
1 in 41
Men who actually die from prostate cancer
~50%
Of men over 70 have microscopic prostate cancer on autopsy — never causing problems
The key insight: Most prostate cancers found through PSA screening are low-grade and slow-growing. The central challenge is distinguishing these from the minority of cancers that genuinely need treatment.

The Gleason/Grade Group System

When prostate cancer is biopsied, pathologists assign a Grade Group (GG) — previously called the Gleason score. This is the single most important measure of how aggressive a cancer is.

Grade Group 1 · Gleason 6
Low-Grade
Cancer cells look almost normal. Grows very slowly. Studies show virtually no metastases or cancer-specific death at 10–15 years if untreated. Active surveillance is the standard of care.
Grade Group 2 · Gleason 3+4=7
Favourable Intermediate
Mostly low-grade with some higher-grade cells. Moderate risk. Active surveillance is often still appropriate, especially for older men or those with limited life expectancy.
Grade Group 3 · Gleason 4+3=7
Unfavourable Intermediate
More high-grade cells than low. Meaningful risk of progression. Treatment is generally recommended for men with >10 years life expectancy.
Grade Group 4–5 · Gleason 8–10
High / Very High Grade
Aggressive cancer cells. Significant risk of metastasis and death. Treatment is strongly recommended for men with adequate life expectancy. Early detection genuinely saves lives here.

Key Trials You Should Know
📊 ERSPC Trial — The Evidence For and Against Screening ›

The European Randomised Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC), involving over 160,000 men, found that PSA screening reduced prostate cancer death by approximately 20–30% over 16 years of follow-up.

However, for every prostate cancer death prevented, screening led to:

  • 27 additional men treated who would never have had symptoms
  • Significant rates of erectile dysfunction (~40–60%) and urinary incontinence (~15–20%) from treatment
  • Anxiety and quality-of-life impacts from diagnosis itself

The PLCO trial (US) showed no benefit — but was later found to be confounded by high rates of screening in the control group.

Schröder FH et al., NEJM 2009, 2012; Hugosson et al., Lancet Oncology 2019
🔬 ProtecT Trial — Surgery vs. Radiation vs. Monitoring ›

The ProtecT trial (UK, 1,643 men, 15 years follow-up) compared radical prostatectomy, radiotherapy, and active monitoring for localised prostate cancer detected by PSA screening.

Key finding: There was no significant difference in prostate cancer death between the three groups (around 3% in all groups at 15 years).

However, active monitoring was associated with a higher rate of cancer progression and metastases at 10 years (33 vs. 22 per 1,000 person-years), though this did not translate to more deaths in the study period.

Treatment complications were significant:

  • Surgery: highest rates of urinary incontinence and erectile dysfunction
  • Radiotherapy: highest rates of bowel problems
  • Active monitoring: highest rates of anxiety but best preservation of urinary and sexual function
Hamdy FC et al., NEJM 2023 (15-year ProtecT follow-up)
🧲 The Role of MRI — A Useful But Context-Dependent Tool ›

mpMRI (multiparametric MRI) is a valuable tool in prostate cancer assessment, but its role depends on clinical context, local availability, and what question is being asked. In Canada, MRI access varies significantly by region, and clinical pathways are appropriately adapted to available resources.

When MRI adds the most value: In men with a PSA in the 3–10 ng/mL range with no other high-risk features, MRI may help identify those who can safely defer biopsy. A clearly negative MRI (PI-RADS 1–2) provides reassurance. However, this requires access to a quality scanner and an experienced radiologist — neither of which is guaranteed in all Canadian centres.

When biopsy is the appropriate direct step: Men with significantly elevated PSA (>10 ng/mL), a suspicious digital rectal exam, rapidly rising PSA, or strong clinical suspicion should not have biopsy delayed by MRI access issues. In these cases, systematic biopsy is the appropriate first step.

How biopsy is performed in practice: When MRI is obtained and shows a suspicious lesion, the standard approach is combined — both a systematic biopsy (sampling the whole prostate in a standardised pattern) and targeted biopsies of the MRI-identified lesion. Targeted-only biopsy risks missing significant cancers elsewhere in the gland and is not standard of care.

PI-RADS scoring: MRI results are reported on a 1–5 scale. PI-RADS 1–2 = very low suspicion; PI-RADS 3 = equivocal (biopsy decision individualised); PI-RADS 4–5 = high suspicion for significant cancer. A PI-RADS 3 in a man with PSA 15 ng/mL should not falsely reassure.

Kasivisvanathan V et al., NEJM 2018 (PRECISION); Ahmed HU et al., Lancet 2017 (PROMIS); CUA PSA Consensus 2023; van der Leest et al., Eur Urol 2019

Step 4 — Investigations and Treatments in Detail
What happens at each step — and what are the realistic benefits and harms

The Investigation Pathway

If you decide to investigate your elevated PSA further, your urologist will determine the most appropriate pathway based on your clinical picture. There is no single fixed sequence — the route depends on your PSA level, clinical findings, and what is available at your centre.

Step A · First
Repeat PSA + Clinical Assessment

A single PSA can vary by up to 25%. A repeat test (ideally after avoiding vigorous exercise, sex, or recent infection for 6 weeks) helps confirm the result. Your doctor will also examine your prostate (DRE). This step costs little and provides important information before any invasive testing.

Step B · Pathway Option 1 — Systematic Biopsy
Transrectal or Transperineal Prostate Biopsy

A systematic biopsy samples the prostate in a standardised pattern (typically 12 cores covering all zones) under local anaesthetic. This is the most established pathway and is appropriate as a direct first step in many clinical scenarios — particularly when PSA is significantly elevated, clinical suspicion is high, or MRI access is limited.

Transperineal vs. transrectal: Transperineal biopsy (through the skin between scrotum and anus) is increasingly preferred as it carries a significantly lower infection risk than the traditional transrectal approach (through the rectal wall). Ask your urologist which technique they use.
✓ Benefits
Definitively diagnoses or rules out cancer
Samples the entire prostate — no area is missed
Provides Gleason grade to guide treatment decisions
Widely available across Canada; no MRI access required
Appropriate direct step when PSA >10 or clinical suspicion is high
✗ Risks
Infection (sepsis): 0.5–2% transrectal; significantly lower with transperineal approach
Bleeding in urine, semen, or rectum (~50% temporary)
Urinary retention (~1–2%)
Pain/discomfort (managed with local anaesthetic)
May detect low-grade cancers that would never cause harm (overdiagnosis)
Step B · Pathway Option 2 — MRI First (Selected Cases)
mpMRI (Multiparametric MRI) — When Available and Appropriate

mpMRI is a non-invasive scan — no needles, no radiation — that provides detailed images of the prostate. It is most useful in specific scenarios: PSA in the 3–10 ng/mL grey zone with lower clinical suspicion, a prior negative systematic biopsy with persistent PSA elevation, or where the urologist wants more information before deciding on biopsy. In Canada, access varies widely — your urologist will advise whether it is appropriate and accessible in your area.

✓ Benefits
Can rule out significant cancer in selected patients (~90% sensitivity)
May avoid biopsy in ~28% of appropriate patients (PRECISION trial)
Guides targeted biopsy to suspicious areas when combined with systematic biopsy
Non-invasive, no recovery time
✗ Limitations
Access limited in many Canadian regions; can cause significant wait time
Can miss some cancers (false negative ~7–10%); a normal MRI does not guarantee no cancer
Result quality depends on scanner quality and radiologist experience
PI-RADS 3 ("equivocal") result can be difficult to interpret and does not remove need for biopsy in higher-risk men
Claustrophobia can be an issue for some patients
Step C · When MRI Has Been Obtained
Combined Systematic + Targeted Biopsy

When an MRI is obtained prior to biopsy and identifies a suspicious lesion (PI-RADS 4–5), the standard practice is to perform both a systematic biopsy and targeted biopsies of the MRI-identified area in the same session. Targeted-only biopsy is not standard of care — it risks missing significant cancers in other parts of the prostate not flagged by MRI.

Why combined biopsy matters: Studies show that systematic biopsy catches clinically significant cancers missed by targeted-only approaches in 5–10% of cases. Conversely, targeted cores catch high-grade disease missed by systematic sampling. Together, they provide the most complete and accurate picture.
If MRI is negative (PI-RADS 1–2): Your urologist will weigh the overall clinical picture. A negative MRI does not absolutely exclude cancer, particularly in men with high PSA or other risk factors. In lower-risk scenarios, monitoring may be appropriate; in higher-risk scenarios, systematic biopsy may still be recommended.
If Cancer Is Found — Treatment Options
Critical context: Finding cancer on biopsy does not automatically mean you need treatment. For many men — especially older men with low-grade cancers — active surveillance is the safest and most appropriate path. All options below have major quality-of-life implications.
🔍 Active Surveillance — Watching and Waiting ›

What it is: Regular monitoring (PSA every 3–6 months, periodic digital rectal exam, repeat MRI where accessible, possible repeat biopsy at 1–2 years) with treatment offered only if the cancer shows signs of progression.

Who it's for: Grade Group 1 (Gleason 6) cancers; selected Grade Group 2 cancers; older men or those with limited life expectancy and higher-grade disease.

97%
15-year prostate cancer-specific survival for GG1 on active surveillance
~50%
Men on active surveillance who remain untreated at 10 years
~30%
Develop anxiety about monitoring, but most prefer it to side effects of treatment
Hamdy et al., NEJM 2023; Klotz et al., Eur Urol 2015; Tosoian et al., Lancet Oncol 2015
🔪 Radical Prostatectomy — Surgical Removal ›

What it is: Surgical removal of the entire prostate, usually by robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery. Typically 2–4 hour procedure; 1–2 days hospital stay; 4–6 weeks recovery.

✓ Benefits
• Removes cancer entirely (when confined to prostate)
• PSA drops to undetectable if successful
• Allows accurate staging of removed tissue
• Curative in ~80–90% of localised disease
✗ Side Effects
• Erectile dysfunction: 40–80% (depends on age, nerve-sparing)
• Urinary incontinence: 5–20% (long-term)
• Climacturia (orgasm-associated leakage)
• Rare: rectal injury, deep vein thrombosis
• General anaesthesia risks
Hamdy et al., NEJM 2023; Resnick et al., NEJM 2013
☢️ Radiotherapy — External Beam or Brachytherapy ›

External Beam Radiation (EBRT): Targeted radiation over 4–7 weeks (or 5 sessions with SBRT). No surgery required. Often combined with hormone therapy for higher-grade cancers.

Brachytherapy: Radioactive seeds implanted into prostate under general anaesthetic. Highly effective for low-risk disease.

✓ Benefits
• Cancer-control rates equivalent to surgery for localised disease
• Better preservation of urinary and erectile function initially
• No surgery; outpatient for most protocols
✗ Side Effects
• Bowel problems: 5–15% (rectal bleeding, frequency — can be long-term)
• Urinary frequency/urgency initially
• Erectile dysfunction: 30–60% (slower onset than surgery)
• Secondary malignancy risk (very small, long-term)
• Hormone therapy side effects if used
💊 Hormone Therapy (ADT) — If Cancer Has Spread ›

Androgen Deprivation Therapy (ADT) lowers testosterone to slow cancer growth. Used for locally advanced or metastatic disease, or combined with radiation for high-risk localised cancer.

Side effects are substantial and often underappreciated:
• Hot flashes (80%), fatigue, mood changes
• Loss of libido and erectile function (nearly universal)
• Bone density loss (osteoporosis) — fracture risk increases
• Cardiovascular risk (metabolic syndrome, diabetes)
• Cognitive effects (memory, concentration)
• Muscle loss and weight gain

For many men, particularly older men, these side effects significantly impact quality of life and may shorten life from other causes.

Step 5 — What Matters Most to You
Medical decisions are not purely about statistics — your values and preferences are equally important

Research consistently shows that different men with identical PSA levels, age, and cancer risk make different — and equally valid — choices based on their personal values. There is no universally "right" answer. The following questions are designed to help you identify what matters most to you.

Questions to Discuss with Your Doctor
  • What is my PSA density (PSA divided by prostate volume on ultrasound)?
  • Has my PSA been trending upward (PSA velocity)? How quickly?
  • Do I have a family history of prostate cancer? (If yes, higher risk applies.)
  • Am I of African-Caribbean descent? (Higher risk population — earlier/more aggressive action may be appropriate.)
  • Based on my PSA level and clinical findings, do you recommend going straight to biopsy, or is an MRI appropriate first?
  • Is MRI accessible at your centre, and would the wait time be reasonable in my situation?
  • What type of biopsy is performed here — transrectal or transperineal? (Transperineal carries lower infection risk.)
  • If MRI is done and shows a lesion, will you perform combined systematic + targeted biopsy?
  • What monitoring interval would you recommend if I choose watchful waiting?
  • What urologist would you refer me to if I choose to investigate further?
