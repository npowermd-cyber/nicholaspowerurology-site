Raised PSA: what happens next

A decision aid from Dr. Power for men referred with a raised PSA. It helps you understand your result, whether more tests make sense for you, the choice between monitoring, an MRI or a biopsy, and what the results mean.

Not every raised PSA needs an MRI or a biopsy. Many things raise PSA, and about 1 in 4 raised results is normal when the test is repeated. Often the first step is a repeat test and keeping watch.

Part 1

Your PSA result

PSA (prostate-specific antigen) is a protein made by the prostate. A small amount gets into the blood. A higher level can be a sign of prostate cancer, but more often it means the prostate is larger, inflamed or irritated.

Why PSA goes up

  • Prostate growth with age (benign prostatic hyperplasia, or BPH). A bigger prostate makes more PSA. This is the most common reason.
  • Inflammation or infection of the prostate (prostatitis) or a bladder infection.
  • Short-term rises. Ejaculation or a long bike ride in the 2 days before the test can raise PSA a little. So can a urinary catheter, a camera test of the bladder (cystoscopy), or a recent prostate biopsy or operation.
  • Prostate cancer. Most prostate cancers that cause a raised PSA are found before they cause any symptoms.

There is no single “normal” PSA. It tends to rise with age and prostate size, so the same number can mean different things for different men. That is why Dr. Power looks at your PSA together with your age, your prostate exam, how your PSA has changed over time and, usually, an MRI.

Checking the PSA again

PSA goes up and down on its own. Guidelines advise repeating a raised PSA before any scan or biopsy.

Repeat PSA

In a Canadian study of men with a PSA over 4, about 25 in 100 had a normal result when the test was repeated.

American guidelines report that 25 to 40 in 100 newly raised PSA results come back normal on repeat testing.

How to get the most accurate repeat test
  • Avoid ejaculation and long bike rides for 2 days before the test.
  • If you have had a urine or prostate infection, wait until it has been treated and you have recovered. Your PSA can take several weeks, often about 6, to settle.
  • If you take finasteride (Proscar, or Propecia for hair loss) or dutasteride (Avodart, Jalyn), tell Dr. Power. These medicines lower PSA by about half, so your result needs to be read differently. Don’t stop them on your own.
  • Antibiotics are not a treatment for a raised PSA on its own. They help only if you have signs of an infection.

Things that raise the chance of prostate cancer

  • Age. Prostate cancer is uncommon before 50 and becomes more common with each decade.
  • Family history. A father or brother with prostate cancer, especially before age 65, or several relatives with prostate, breast, ovarian or pancreatic cancer.
  • African or Caribbean ancestry. Black men are more likely to get prostate cancer and to get it younger.
  • Inherited gene changes such as BRCA2. If someone in your family has tested positive, tell us; you may be offered genetic counselling.

Let Dr. Power know about any of these. They can change how closely you are followed and whether a biopsy is advised.

Who pays for the tests

In Ontario, OHIP pays for a PSA test when your doctor orders it because prostate cancer is suspected (for example, because of your family history, ancestry, an exam finding or symptoms) or to follow prostate cancer that has been diagnosed. A PSA done only as a screening test for a man with no symptoms is not covered, and the lab will charge a fee.

The MRI and biopsy, when Dr. Power orders them, are covered by OHIP. Some newer blood and urine tests that can add information (such as PHI, 4Kscore or Stockholm3) are not publicly funded in Ontario and are not routinely needed.

The bigger picture: benefits and harms of finding prostate cancer early

The largest trial of PSA testing, the European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC), followed about 160,000 men for 23 years. Men offered regular PSA tests were about 13% less likely to die of prostate cancer. In real numbers, for every 456 men offered testing, 1 fewer man died of prostate cancer.

The main harm is overdiagnosis: finding cancers that are so slow-growing they would never have caused problems. In the same trial, for each prostate cancer death prevented, about 12 more men were diagnosed with prostate cancer in the screened group than in the unscreened group. Not all of these extra diagnoses were harmless cancers, but many were, and in the past most men diagnosed had surgery or radiation, with side effects that some did not benefit from.

Today’s approach aims to keep the benefit and cut the harm: testing mainly the men most likely to benefit, using an MRI where it helps, so fewer men need a biopsy and fewer harmless cancers are found. When a slow-growing cancer is found, most men are offered active surveillance (regular checks) instead of treatment.

Part 2

Your age, your health, and whether to test

Finding prostate cancer early helps some men and not others. Whether a raised PSA needs more tests depends a great deal on your age and general health.

Why age and health matter so much

Most prostate cancers grow slowly. When a cancer needs treatment, it usually takes many years to cause harm. So finding it early helps most when a man is likely to live at least another 10 years. For a man who is older, or who has serious heart, lung, kidney or other health problems, a slow-growing cancer found by a PSA test is unlikely ever to affect his life, while more tests and treatment can cause real problems.

What Canadian guidance says about PSA testing

  • Canadian Urological Association (2022): offer PSA testing, after talking about the pros and cons, to men aged 50 to 69 who are likely to live more than 10 years, starting at 45 for men at higher risk (for example, a strong family history or African or Caribbean ancestry). Stop at 70 for most men; men in excellent health at 70 may decide with their doctor to continue. Don’t test men who are likely to live less than about 10 years.
  • Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care (2014): recommended against routine PSA screening for all men. The recommendation was strong for men under 55 and men 70 and over, and weaker for men 55 to 69, because some men in that age group value the small chance of benefit. (The Task Force was replaced in 2026 by a new national committee, which had not issued new advice on prostate screening when this page was written.)

If your PSA was done anyway

Many men over 69, and men with serious health problems, have a PSA test as part of routine blood work, even though guidelines don’t recommend it. If that happened to you, you have done nothing wrong, and a raised result does not mean you must have an MRI or a biopsy.

If a slow-growing cancer would be unlikely to affect your life, the wiser choice is often to stop PSA testing, or to recheck it only occasionally, and to look further only if there are warning signs: a clearly abnormal prostate exam, a very high or fast-rising PSA, or symptoms such as new bone pain.

Stopping PSA tests is a real, reasonable choice. You can ask your family doctor not to order more PSA tests, or to check with you first. Stopping PSA tests doesn’t mean stopping care: urinary problems can still be treated, and any new symptoms should still be checked.

Part 3

Your route: monitor, MRI or biopsy

There is no single path for every man. Prostate MRI is a limited resource, and in London the wait for one is usually about 3 months. So Dr. Power will choose a route with you, based on your PSA results, your prostate exam, your age and health, and what matters to you.

First: check again

At your first visit, Dr. Power will ask about your health, urinary symptoms, family history and medicines, and usually examine your prostate. This is a digital rectal exam: a gloved finger in the back passage to feel the back of the prostate. It takes less than a minute. A repeat PSA is often arranged too (see Part 1). Then one of these routes is chosen together.

Monitor

Repeat PSA tests and keep watch, with an MRI or biopsy only if something changes.

What happens
  • A repeat PSA, often in about 6 to 12 weeks, to confirm the result. About 1 in 4 raised PSAs is normal on repeat testing.
  • Then PSA tests at regular intervals, often every 3 to 12 months, sometimes with a repeat prostate exam.
  • Dr. Power looks at how fast your PSA is changing (sometimes called PSA velocity or doubling time), along with your exam and any symptoms. A single change is not enough on its own to call for a biopsy, but a steady rise can lead to an MRI or biopsy.
Often the right choice when
  • the PSA rise is modest, or may have another cause;
  • the prostate exam is normal;
  • your age or health mean that finding a slow-growing cancer would not help you; or
  • you prefer to avoid tests for now, after understanding the trade-offs.
Trade-offs
  • No scan or biopsy, and much less chance of finding a cancer that would never matter.
  • Some uncertainty between tests.
  • If there is a significant cancer, it would be found later rather than now. For most men this is safe because most prostate cancers grow slowly, which is why the PSA is rechecked.

MRI first

A scan of the prostate to decide whether a biopsy is worthwhile.

An MRI looks for areas likely to be clinically significant cancer: a cancer that is likely to grow and may need treatment (Grade Group 2 or higher). It finds more of these, and fewer harmless cancers, than going straight to a standard biopsy (see Part 4).

Usually chosen when
  • there is real concern, such as a PSA that stays raised or keeps rising, a strong family history, or a high PSA for the size of the prostate, but no obvious need for a biopsy right away; and
  • you are healthy enough that you would want treatment if a significant cancer were found. MRI is kept for the men most likely to benefit from it.
The wait

In London, the wait for a prostate MRI is usually about 3 months. For most men this is safe, because most prostate cancers grow slowly. If you notice any of the warning signs below while you wait, don’t wait for the scan: get help as described.

Straight to biopsy

A biopsy without waiting for an MRI, when the need is obvious.

Usually chosen when

Waiting about 3 months for an MRI would not change the decision. For example:

  • the prostate feels clearly abnormal on exam (hard or irregular);
  • the PSA is very high, or rising quickly;
  • there are symptoms that suggest the cancer may have spread, such as new bone pain;
  • you can’t have an MRI (for example, some pacemakers or severe claustrophobia).

Sometimes other scans (such as a CT, bone scan or PSMA PET scan) are done first or at the same time, to check whether a cancer has spread.

What the guidelines say

Canadian and Ontario guidance prefers an MRI before a first biopsy where a good-quality MRI is available and the result would change what is done. European guidelines say a biopsy without MRI is reasonable when the exam clearly suggests cancer that has grown outside the prostate. In practice, MRI access is limited, so the route is matched to how urgent the situation is.

Trade-offs
  • A faster answer when one is clearly needed.
  • Without an MRI to guide it, a standard biopsy can miss some significant cancers and find more slow-growing ones. In the situations above, the chance of a significant cancer is high enough that this matters less.
  • The usual small risks of a biopsy (see Part 5).

Stop PSA testing

No more PSA tests, MRIs or biopsies, unless new symptoms appear.

Often a sensible choice when
  • you are older, or have serious health problems, so a slow-growing cancer would be unlikely to affect your life (see Part 2);
  • you would not want treatment even if a cancer were found; and
  • there are no warning signs.

Ask your family doctor not to order more PSA tests. Keep reporting new symptoms, such as bone pain or trouble passing urine.

While you wait: when to get help

Go to an emergency department if you have:

  • trouble passing urine: none at all, or only a few drops, with a full, painful bladder;
  • new weakness or numbness in your legs;
  • new loss of control of your bladder or bowels.

Call our office at 1-888-909-2786 if you notice new bone or back pain that doesn’t go away, or blood in your urine. For what to watch for after a biopsy, see Part 5.

Right now, I am:
Newly referred with a raised PSA

Your family doctor found a PSA that was higher than expected. That is a reason to look more closely, not a diagnosis.

  • Dr. Power will ask about your health, symptoms, family history and medicines, and usually examine your prostate.
  • Together you will choose a route: monitor with repeat PSA tests, an MRI first, straight to biopsy if the need is obvious, or, for some men, stop testing.
  • Your age and health make a big difference. See Part 2.
Having repeat PSA tests (monitoring)

This is a common and safe first step. Many raised PSAs settle, and many that stay a little raised never lead to a problem.

  • Make each test accurate (see Part 1), and ask when your next test is due and who will check the result.
  • An MRI or biopsy is usually considered if the PSA keeps rising, the exam changes, or new symptoms appear.
  • If you are older or in poorer health, ask whether you need to keep testing at all.
Waiting for, or just had, my MRI

In London the wait is usually about 3 months, which is safe for most men. The MRI report gives a PI-RADS score from 1 to 5 for how likely any area is to be a significant cancer. It also measures your prostate, which is used to work out your PSA density.

  • Dr. Power will go over the result with you and talk about whether a biopsy is worthwhile.
  • Meanwhile, know the warning signs below, and think about what matters to you in Part 7.
  • See what the scores mean in Part 4.
My MRI was PI-RADS 1 or 2 (not suspicious)

This is reassuring. Significant cancer is unlikely but not impossible: about 7 in 100 men with this result who have a biopsy anyway are found to have one.

  • After talking it over, many men in this situation choose to skip a biopsy for now and keep checking their PSA, especially when their PSA density is under 0.15 and there is no strong family history.
  • A biopsy may still be advised if your PSA density is 0.15 or more, your exam is abnormal, your PSA keeps rising, or you have strong risk factors.
  • If you skip the biopsy, a plan for repeat PSA tests (and sometimes a repeat MRI) is important.
My MRI was PI-RADS 3 (unclear)

The scan found an area that could be either harmless or a cancer. Most PI-RADS 3 areas are not significant cancer: about 11 in 100 men with this result who have a biopsy are found to have one.

  • In Ontario, PI-RADS 3 counts as a positive MRI, and a biopsy (targeted plus systematic samples) is usually recommended.
  • European guidelines say a biopsy can be skipped, with PSA checks instead, when PSA density is under 0.10 and there is no strong family history. You can discuss this with Dr. Power.
  • Your own preferences matter here. See Part 7.
My MRI was PI-RADS 4 or 5 (suspicious)

The scan shows an area that is likely to be a significant cancer. A biopsy is usually recommended to find out for sure and to see how aggressive it is.

  • The biopsy aims needles at the area seen on the MRI, usually with systematic samples from the rest of the prostate as well.
  • Not every PI-RADS 4 or 5 area turns out to be cancer.
  • If you are older or have serious health problems, it is reasonable to ask whether the result of a biopsy would change what you would do.
  • Read about the biopsy in Part 5.
Booked for a biopsy

Read Part 5 for how to prepare, what is normal afterward and when to get help.

  • Tell us well ahead if you take blood thinners, have a heart valve or joint replacement, or have had an infection after a previous biopsy.
  • Know the warning signs of infection. Fever or chills after a biopsy need an emergency department right away.
My biopsy showed no cancer

Good news, though no test is perfect. A biopsy can miss a small area of cancer.

  • You will usually keep having PSA tests. Ask how often, and what result would make you want to look again.
  • A repeat MRI or biopsy is considered only if your PSA keeps rising or something else changes.
My biopsy showed atypical cells (ASAP) or PIN

These are not cancer, but they are worth knowing about. See Part 6.

  • ASAP (atypical small acinar proliferation): cells that look suspicious but are not clearly cancer. Further tests, often a repeat MRI or biopsy, are usually advised.
  • PIN (prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia): abnormal-looking cells inside the glands. A single small area usually needs no extra biopsy, just your usual PSA checks.
I have Grade Group 1 cancer (Gleason 3+3=6)

This is the slowest-growing kind of prostate cancer. It very rarely spreads, and for most men the recommended plan is active surveillance: regular PSA tests, MRIs and occasional biopsies, with treatment only if it changes.

  • See what the grade means in Part 6.
  • The prostate cancer decision aid explains active surveillance and the other options in detail.
I have Grade Group 2 or higher cancer

This is called clinically significant cancer. It is more likely to grow over time, and treatment is often worth discussing. For some men with a small amount of Grade Group 2, active surveillance is still an option.

  • You may need more scans to check whether the cancer is only inside the prostate.
  • The prostate cancer decision aid helps you find your risk group and compare surveillance, surgery and radiation.
  • There is usually time to read, ask questions and decide. A few weeks is safe for almost everyone.

Part 4

The MRI scan

A prostate MRI uses a strong magnet, not X-rays, to make detailed pictures of the prostate. Guidance from Ontario Health (Cancer Care Ontario), endorsed by the Canadian Urological Association, recommends an MRI before a first biopsy for men at higher risk who would want treatment if a significant cancer were found, where a good-quality MRI is available. An MRI first finds more of the cancers that matter and fewer of the ones that don’t. But MRI time is limited, and in London the wait is usually about 3 months, so it is kept for the men most likely to benefit (see Part 3).

What happens at the MRI
  • Our office requests the scan. The hospital’s MRI department contacts you with the date and any preparation instructions. Follow these closely, because they help make the pictures clear.
  • You lie on your back on a table that slides into a tunnel-shaped scanner. It is noisy, so you get earplugs or headphones. The scan takes about 30 to 45 minutes and doesn’t hurt.
  • Most scans include an injection of a dye (contrast) into a vein in your arm, which helps show any abnormal areas. Some centres use a shorter scan without dye.
  • Before booking, tell us if you have a pacemaker, other implanted device, metal fragments, kidney disease, or a past reaction to MRI dye.
  • If you are claustrophobic, tell us ahead of time. A mild sedative can often be arranged; you would then need someone to drive you home.
Downsides of an MRI
  • False alarms. Many suspicious areas turn out not to be significant cancer. About 6 in 10 men with a PI-RADS 4 area who have a biopsy don’t have significant cancer (see the figures below). A suspicious result can mean worry and a biopsy that finds nothing important.
  • Missed cancers. A normal-looking MRI doesn’t rule out significant cancer completely (see “What a reassuring MRI can and can’t tell you” below).
  • Other findings. The scan sometimes shows something unrelated in the pelvis, such as a cyst or an enlarged lymph node. Most are harmless, but some need more tests.
  • The contrast dye (gadolinium) is very safe for most people. Serious allergic reactions are rare. It is used with extra care, or not at all, in people with severe kidney disease. Tiny amounts can stay in the body for a long time; no harm from this has been shown.
  • Waiting. There can be a wait of weeks for a prostate MRI. For most men with a raised PSA, waiting this long is safe. Ask us if you are worried about the wait.

What usually happens after each score

This follows the Ontario Health (Cancer Care Ontario) guideline that the Canadian Urological Association endorses:

  • PI-RADS 3, 4 or 5 counts as a positive MRI. A biopsy is usually recommended, taking targeted samples from the area and systematic samples from the rest of the prostate at the same time.
  • PI-RADS 1 or 2 is a negative MRI. After talking it over, many men choose to skip a biopsy and keep having PSA tests. A biopsy may still be offered if your PSA density is 0.15 or more, your exam is abnormal, or you have strong risk factors.
Where other guidelines differ
  • For PI-RADS 3, European guidelines say a biopsy can be skipped, with PSA checks instead, when PSA density is under 0.10 and there is no strong family history. This is something you can discuss with Dr. Power; it is not the usual approach in Ontario.
  • For PI-RADS 1 or 2, European guidelines use a PSA density cut-off of 0.20 rather than 0.15 when deciding whether a biopsy can be skipped.
  • American and European guidelines allow targeted samples alone, or with a few extra samples around the area, instead of a full set of systematic samples. This finds fewer slow-growing cancers but may miss a few significant ones.

MRI first, compared with going straight to biopsy

Where the numbers come from

PRECISION (2018) was a trial in 500 men with a raised PSA (up to 20) who had never had a biopsy. Half went straight to a standard biopsy. The other half had an MRI first, and had a biopsy only if the MRI showed a suspicious area (PI-RADS 3 to 5), aimed at that area. In PRECISION only targeted samples were taken; in Ontario, systematic samples are usually added.

What a reassuring MRI can and can’t tell you

MRI is good, but not perfect. There are two ways to look at this:

  • Your own chance. Among men with a PI-RADS 1 or 2 scan who had a biopsy anyway, about 7 in 100 had a significant cancer (the figure above). So a normal MRI makes significant cancer unlikely, but not impossible.
  • Across a group of men. Canadian guidance estimates that if every man with a normal MRI skipped the biopsy, about 8 to 24 of every 100 significant cancers in that group would not be found at first. Regular PSA tests afterward are meant to catch these.

That is why, if you skip a biopsy, your PSA keeps being checked, and why a biopsy may still be offered if your PSA density is high, your exam is abnormal or you have strong risk factors.

Work out your PSA density

PSA density is your PSA divided by the size of your prostate. A large prostate makes more PSA, so a PSA of 6 means something different in a large prostate than in a small one. Doctors use PSA density together with the MRI score to decide whether a biopsy is worthwhile.

ng/mL

Use the result closest to the date of your scan.

mL (or cc)

From your MRI or ultrasound report. It may be written as mL, cc, cm³ or grams; these are the same for this purpose. Enter just the number.

My MRI score, if I know it (optional)

To work it out yourself: divide your PSA (in ng/mL) by your prostate volume (in mL). For example, a PSA of 6 and a prostate of 40 mL gives 6 ÷ 40 = 0.15. Round to two decimal places.

  • Under 0.10: lower. Significant cancer is less likely.
  • 0.10 to 0.14: in between.
  • 0.15 or more: higher. A biopsy is more often advised, even if the MRI looks normal.
Things to keep in mind about PSA density
  • It is a guide, not a diagnosis, and not a risk score. Guidelines warn against using it alone.
  • Prostate size is an estimate, and MRI and ultrasound can give somewhat different numbers, so a result close to a cut-off could fall on either side of it.
  • If you take finasteride or dutasteride, PSA density is harder to read. Ask Dr. Power.
  • A PSA raised by a recent infection, ejaculation or a procedure will give a falsely high result.
  • This page uses the 0.15 cut-off mentioned in Canadian and American guidelines. Canadian guidance notes that this cut-off has not been fully proven. European guidelines use 0.20 with a normal MRI and 0.10 with an unclear (PI-RADS 3) MRI.

Part 5

The biopsy

A biopsy is the only test that can show for certain whether there is cancer and how aggressive it is. A thin needle takes small strips of tissue (called cores), each thinner than the lead of a pencil and a centimetre or two long, which are examined under a microscope.

What is sampled

  • Targeted samples from any area seen on the MRI. The MRI pictures are lined up with live ultrasound images so the needle can be aimed at the area.
  • Systematic samples: usually about 10 to 12 cores spread evenly through the prostate, to catch cancer the MRI may have missed. In Ontario, these are usually taken along with the targeted samples when the MRI is positive, and on their own if a biopsy is done without an MRI or after a normal MRI. Adding them finds a few more significant cancers, but also more slow-growing ones.

Two ways to do a biopsy

Transperineal through the skin

The needle goes in through the skin between the scrotum and the anus. An ultrasound probe in the back passage guides it.

  • Infection is less common, because the needle doesn’t pass through the bowel. Antibiotics are often not needed.
  • Done with a local anaesthetic (freezing), sometimes with sedation or a general anaesthetic.
  • Reaches all parts of the prostate well, including the front.
  • More men find it uncomfortable or embarrassing at the time, and it takes a little longer.

Transrectal through the rectum

An ultrasound probe goes into the back passage (rectum), and the needle passes through the bowel wall into the prostate.

  • The traditional method, done with local anaesthetic, and usually quick.
  • Infection is more likely, because bacteria from the bowel can be carried into the prostate. Antibiotics are always given, sometimes chosen after a swab of the rectum.
  • More bleeding from the back passage, and blood in the semen can last longer.

Infection after a biopsy is uncommon with either method. How much lower it is with the transperineal method depends on the study:

What the largest recent trial found

In TRANSLATE, 1,126 men in the UK were randomly assigned to a transperineal biopsy under local anaesthetic or a transrectal biopsy. Out of 100 men:

  • Significant cancer found: 60 with transperineal, 54 with transrectal.
  • Found it painful or embarrassing straight afterward: 38 with transperineal, 27 with transrectal.
  • Symptoms lasting more than a week: 18 with transperineal, 25 with transrectal.
  • Serious side effects: 2 with transperineal, 4 with transrectal.

In PREVENT, apart from infection, side effects were low and similar with both methods.

European guidelines recommend the transperineal method because of its lower infection risk. American guidelines accept either method. Ask Dr. Power which method will be used for you, and why.

Before your biopsy

  • Blood thinners. Tell us as soon as your biopsy is booked if you take warfarin, apixaban (Eliquis), rivaroxaban (Xarelto), dabigatran, edoxaban, clopidogrel (Plavix), ticagrelor or similar medicines. You may need to stop them for a few days, but only with advice from us or the doctor who prescribed them. Low-dose aspirin is often continued.
  • Antibiotics. If you are given an antibiotic, take it exactly as instructed.
  • Tell us if you have had an infection after a previous biopsy, have recently taken antibiotics or travelled outside Canada, or have an artificial heart valve or joint.
  • Getting home. If you have sedation or a general anaesthetic, someone must drive you home.

  • A few men have trouble passing urine for a short time, usually those with a large prostate.

When to get help urgently

Go to the nearest emergency department right away (or call 911 if you feel very unwell) if, in the days after a biopsy, you have:

  • a fever of 38°C or higher, shaking chills, or a sudden feeling of being very unwell, even if Tylenol brings the fever down (a blood infection after a biopsy can become serious within hours);
  • trouble passing urine: none at all, or only a few drops, with a full, painful bladder;
  • heavy bleeding: urine like red wine or with clots, or more than a small amount of blood from the back passage.

Tell the emergency team you have just had a prostate biopsy. For other questions or concerns after your biopsy, call our office at 1-888-909-2786.

Getting your results

The samples are examined by a pathologist (a doctor who diagnoses disease from tissue). The report usually takes a couple of weeks. Our office will arrange a visit or a call with Dr. Power to go over it. If you haven’t heard from us within about 3 weeks, call the office.

Part 6

Understanding your biopsy result

The pathology report says whether cancer was found and, if so, how aggressive it looks. Here is what the main terms mean.

No cancer found (benign)

This is reassuring, but a biopsy samples only a small part of the prostate, so it can miss a cancer. You will usually keep having PSA tests. Guidelines advise against stopping PSA checks because of one negative biopsy. If your PSA keeps rising, a repeat MRI or biopsy may be suggested. The report may also mention inflammation or atrophy, which are common and harmless. Inflammation can also raise PSA.

Findings that are not cancer but need follow-up

  • ASAP (atypical small acinar proliferation): a few cells that look suspicious, but too few to say for sure. Because cancer is found on a later biopsy in a fair number of these men, further testing is usually advised, often a repeat MRI or biopsy.
  • High-grade PIN (prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia): abnormal-looking cells that stay inside the normal glands. If it is in only one sample, no repeat biopsy is usually needed. If it is in several samples, Dr. Power may suggest more tests, such as an MRI.

Cancer found: what the grade means

The pathologist looks at how much the cancer cells differ from normal prostate tissue and gives the two most common patterns a score from 3 to 5. Added together, these make the Gleason score. Today, Gleason scores are grouped into five Grade Groups, which are easier to understand. The higher the Grade Group, the more aggressive the cancer.

Grade Group 1 Gleason 3+3=6

The slowest-growing kind. It very rarely spreads, even after many years. Most men are offered active surveillance, not treatment.

Grade Group 2 Gleason 3+4=7

Mostly slow-growing, with a smaller amount of a more active pattern. Treatment is often discussed; active surveillance suits some men with a small amount.

Grade Group 3 Gleason 4+3=7

Mostly the more active pattern. Treatment is usually recommended for men who are otherwise healthy.

Grade Group 4 Gleason 8

More aggressive. Treatment is usually recommended, and more scans are done first.

Grade Group 5 Gleason 9 or 10

The most aggressive kind. Treatment is usually recommended, often combining more than one type.

Doctors call Grade Group 2 and higher clinically significant cancer, because these are the cancers that can grow and spread if left alone. They are what the MRI and biopsy are mainly trying to find.

Other things the report may mention
  • Number of cores with cancer, and how much of each core contains cancer (for example, “5% of 1 core” or “80% of 4 cores”). Less cancer usually means lower risk.
  • Cribriform pattern or intraductal carcinoma: patterns linked with more aggressive behaviour in Grade Group 2 or higher cancer, which can affect whether active surveillance is suitable.
  • Perineural invasion: cancer next to a small nerve inside the prostate. It is common and doesn’t mean the cancer has spread.

If cancer is found: what comes next

  • Dr. Power will put your result together with your PSA, MRI and exam to work out your risk group.
  • For higher-risk cancers, you may need more scans (such as a PSMA PET scan, CT or bone scan) to check whether the cancer has spread outside the prostate. Most men with Grade Group 1 or a small amount of Grade Group 2 don’t need them.
  • Then you choose a plan together: active surveillance, surgery, radiation or, for some men, watchful waiting. A diagnosis does not automatically mean treatment.
  • You usually have time. Taking a few weeks to read, ask questions and talk with your family is safe for almost everyone.

What matters to you

At each step, there is a choice between finding out more now and keeping watch with PSA tests, or, for some men, stopping testing. Men in the same situation make different choices, and both can be reasonable. Think about how important each of these is to you.

Finding out for sure whether I have a cancer that needs treatment
Not missing a cancer that could grow and spread
Avoiding a biopsy unless it is really needed
Avoiding the risks of a biopsy, such as infection and bleeding
Not being told I have a slow-growing cancer that would never harm me
Not living with uncertainty between tests
Avoiding tests and treatment that may not change how long or how well I live
Keeping tests, hospital visits and time off to a minimum
