Prostate cancer: choosing your treatment

A decision aid from Dr. Power to help you understand your options, compare them side by side, and work out which one fits what matters most to you.

You usually have time. Most prostate cancers grow slowly. Taking a few weeks to read, ask questions and talk with your family is safe for almost everyone.
There is often no single right answer. For many men, the main options control the cancer about equally well. They differ mostly in side effects, time and what daily life looks like afterward.

Part 2

Your options

These are the main ways prostate cancer that hasn’t spread is managed. Once you enter your risk group in Part 1, this section shows which options are usually offered for it.

Active surveillance

Watch the cancer closely with regular tests, and treat it only if it starts to change.

Many prostate cancers, especially Grade Group 1, grow so slowly that they never cause harm. Active surveillance lets you avoid treatment and its side effects for as long as it is safe, while keeping the chance to be cured if the cancer becomes more active. It is not “doing nothing”: it is a structured plan of checks.

What happens
  • A PSA blood test about every 3 to 6 months.
  • An MRI of the prostate, usually within the first year and then every few years.
  • A repeat biopsy, often about a year after diagnosis and then every few years, or sooner if the PSA or MRI changes.
  • If tests show the cancer is growing or becoming more aggressive, you are offered treatment, usually surgery or radiation.
What to expect
  • No treatment side effects while you stay on surveillance. Erections, bladder and bowel are affected only by normal aging.
  • About 1 in 3 men on active surveillance go on to have treatment within about 10 years, usually because a test shows the cancer has changed. Treatment at that point still works well for most.
  • Biopsies carry a small risk of infection or bleeding.
  • Some men find living with an untreated cancer stressful, especially around test results. For others it soon becomes routine.
Who it suits

The recommended first choice for most men with low-risk cancer. A reasonable option for some men with favourable intermediate-risk cancer, especially when there is only a small amount of Grade Group 2. It is not usually advised for higher-risk cancers.

Surgery (radical prostatectomy)

Remove the whole prostate and the seminal vesicles in an operation.

The surgeon removes the prostate through several small cuts, usually with a robotic system. For higher-risk cancers, nearby lymph nodes are removed too. The removed tissue is examined in the lab, which gives the most complete picture of the cancer.

What happens
  • A general anaesthetic and an operation of about 2 to 4 hours.
  • Most men go home the next day.
  • A urinary catheter (tube) stays in for about 1 to 2 weeks while the new join between the bladder and urethra heals.
  • Most men return to desk work in 2 to 4 weeks. No heavy lifting for about 6 weeks.
  • Afterward, PSA blood tests should show almost no PSA, because the prostate is gone.
Side effects
  • Leaking urine is expected at first and improves over weeks to months. Pelvic floor exercises help. Most men are dry or need at most one light pad a day by a year. A smaller number have lasting leakage; treatments are available.
  • Erections are usually weaker after surgery and recover slowly over 1 to 2 years, if they recover. Recovery depends a lot on your age, how good your erections were before, and whether the nerves can be spared. Pills, injections and other treatments can help.
  • Orgasm without semen. Orgasm is still possible, but no semen comes out (“dry orgasm”), so natural conception is no longer possible. Some men leak a little urine at orgasm, or notice the penis is slightly shorter.
  • Bowel function is not usually affected.
  • Risks of any operation: bleeding, infection, blood clots, hernia, and rarely injury to nearby organs.
Good to know
  • The lab report shows exactly how far the cancer had spread inside the prostate.
  • If PSA rises again later, radiation is still available as a second treatment.
  • For high-risk cancers, some men need radiation, hormone therapy or both after surgery.
  • Surgery tends to help men with a large prostate and bothersome urinary symptoms pass urine more easily.

External beam radiation

Precisely aimed X-ray beams treat the prostate from outside the body, sometimes with hormone therapy.

In London, radiation is given at the Verspeeten Family Cancer Centre at Victoria Hospital (formerly the London Regional Cancer Program), planned by a radiation oncologist. Each treatment takes about 15 to 30 minutes and doesn’t hurt; it feels like having an X-ray. You don’t need to stay in hospital.

What happens
  • Planning first: a CT or MRI scan, and often small gold markers or a gel “spacer” placed near the prostate to make treatment more precise and protect the rectum.
  • Treatment on weekdays. Depending on your cancer and the technique, this may be as few as 5 sessions (stereotactic radiation) or about 20 to 28 sessions over 4 to 6 weeks.
  • Hormone therapy is often added for intermediate- and high-risk cancers. Its length depends on your risk group.
  • PSA blood tests afterward. PSA falls slowly over 1 to 2 years.
Side effects
  • During and just after treatment: needing to pee more often or urgently, burning, bowel urgency or looser stools, and tiredness. These usually settle within weeks to a few months.
  • Erections tend to weaken gradually over the following years. Hormone therapy adds to this while you are on it.
  • Leaking urine is uncommon. Some men have long-term urinary frequency, urgency or, occasionally, blood in the urine.
  • Bowel: a small number of men have lasting bowel urgency or rectal bleeding.
  • A very small increase in the chance of a second cancer in the bladder or rectum many years later.
About hormone therapy

Hormone therapy (androgen deprivation therapy) lowers testosterone, which makes radiation work better for intermediate- and high-risk cancers. It is usually an injection every 1 to 6 months. While you are on it, common side effects are hot flushes, tiredness, low sex drive, erection problems, weight gain and loss of muscle, and mood changes. Over longer periods it can thin the bones and affect heart and blood sugar health. Most effects wear off gradually after it stops. Exercise helps a great deal.

Good to know
  • No operation or general anaesthetic, and you can usually keep working.
  • Daily trips to the cancer centre can be hard if you live far from London; 5-session treatment is an option for some men.
  • If the cancer comes back after radiation, further treatment is possible, but surgery afterward is more difficult.

Brachytherapy (internal radiation)

Radiation sources placed inside the prostate deliver a high dose right where it’s needed.

Thin needles guided by ultrasound place the radiation directly into the prostate, under anaesthetic. There are two kinds:

  • Seed (low-dose-rate) brachytherapy: tiny radioactive seeds are left in the prostate permanently and give off radiation over a few months.
  • High-dose-rate brachytherapy: a strong source is placed through temporary tubes for a few minutes, then removed. Nothing stays in the body.

Brachytherapy can be used on its own for lower-risk cancers, or as a “boost” together with external beam radiation for higher-risk cancers.

What happens
  • A day procedure, or an overnight stay, with a spinal or general anaesthetic. High-dose-rate treatment may be given in 1 or 2 sessions.
  • Most men are back to normal activities within a few days.
  • When used as a boost, it is combined with a course of external beam radiation and often hormone therapy.
Side effects
  • Urinary irritation is the main effect: frequency, urgency, a weaker stream and burning, often for several months, especially after seeds.
  • A small number of men (roughly 5 in 100) can’t pass urine for a while and need a catheter.
  • Erections tend to weaken gradually over the following years.
  • Bowel effects such as rectal bleeding are uncommon.
  • Leaking urine is uncommon.
Good to know
  • It is not suitable for everyone, for example men with a very large prostate or significant urinary symptoms before treatment.
  • After seed brachytherapy, there are a few simple precautions for a short time, such as avoiding long close contact with pregnant women and young children.
  • If the cancer comes back afterward, further treatment is possible, but surgery is more difficult.

Watchful waiting

No curative treatment. Keep an eye on your health, and treat symptoms if they appear.

For some men, especially older men or those with other serious health problems, a slow-growing prostate cancer is unlikely to cause problems in their lifetime. Watchful waiting avoids the side effects of surgery and radiation entirely.

How it differs from active surveillance
  • There are no routine biopsies or MRIs. The aim is not to cure the cancer later, but to keep you well and comfortable.
  • PSA tests and check-ups are less frequent.
  • If the cancer causes symptoms, such as bone pain or trouble passing urine, treatments such as hormone therapy can control it, often for many years.
Who it suits

Men who are likely to live less than about 10 years because of age or other health problems, or men who strongly prefer to avoid curative treatment and understand the trade-offs. It can be a reasonable choice at any risk level when life expectancy is limited.

Not usually recommended for your risk group

These options are not usually offered for your risk group, but you can still read about them. Ask Dr. Power if you’d like to know why.

Other treatments you may read about

Focal therapies treat only part of the prostate, for example with high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) or freezing (cryotherapy). Their long-term results are less well known than those of surgery and radiation, and in Ontario they are mostly available in clinical trials or at private cost. Ask us if you are interested. We can also tell you about clinical trials you may be eligible for.

Part 3

Compare your options

Here the options are compared one topic at a time. Once you have entered your risk group, only the options usually offered for it are shown.

Where the numbers come from

The “out of 100 men” figures come from ProtecT, a large UK trial in which 1,643 men with prostate cancer found by PSA testing were randomly assigned to active monitoring, surgery or radiation (with 3 to 6 months of hormone therapy), and followed for 15 years. Most had low-risk or intermediate-risk cancer.

Keep in mind: these men were treated between 1999 and 2009. Surgery and radiation techniques have improved since then, and “active monitoring” in the trial was less thorough than today’s active surveillance with MRI. Your own chances also depend on your age and how well things work before treatment. Use the numbers to compare options, not as exact predictions.

Controlling the cancer

Out of 100 men in ProtecT, after 15 years:

Active monitoring

3 died of prostate cancer

9 had cancer spread

Surgery

2 died of prostate cancer

5 had cancer spread

Radiation

3 died of prostate cancer

5 had cancer spread

The difference in deaths from prostate cancer was too small to be sure it was real. Men on monitoring were more likely to have the cancer spread, which usually meant starting long-term hormone therapy. About 1 in 4 men on monitoring were still alive and had never needed treatment after 15 years. Over the same time, about 21 to 23 in 100 men in every group died of other causes.

  • Active surveillance: best suited to low-risk cancer. Today’s surveillance, with MRI and planned biopsies, is designed to catch change earlier than the monitoring in ProtecT.
  • Surgery and radiation control the cancer about equally well when each is matched to the risk group.
  • Brachytherapy gives results similar to external beam radiation for the cancers it is suited to, and as a boost it improves cancer control for higher-risk cancers.
  • Watchful waiting accepts a higher chance that the cancer will grow, in exchange for avoiding treatment, when other health issues are likely to matter more.

Leaking urine

Out of 100 men in ProtecT, the number using pads for leaking urine:

Active monitoring

4 at 6 months

8 at 6 years

Surgery

46 at 6 months

17 at 6 years

Radiation

5 at 6 months

4 at 6 years

  • Surgery: leakage is expected at first and improves over the first year. Most of the ProtecT operations were open surgery; with modern robotic surgery in experienced hands, fewer men need pads in the long term, but some leakage remains the main trade-off of surgery.
  • External radiation: leakage is uncommon, but some men have lasting urgency or need to pee often, including at night.
  • Brachytherapy: leakage is uncommon, but urinary irritation for several months is common, and a few men need a catheter for a time.
  • Active surveillance and watchful waiting: no treatment effect. Urinary symptoms can still come with age or prostate enlargement.

Erections and sex

Before treatment, 67 in 100 men in ProtecT had erections firm enough for intercourse. Out of 100 men, the number who had them afterward:

Active monitoring

52 at 6 months

30 at 6 years

Surgery

12 at 6 months

17 at 6 years

Radiation

22 at 6 months

27 at 6 years

Erections decline with age in every group, which is why the numbers fall even with monitoring.

  • Surgery: the biggest drop is right after surgery, with slow partial recovery. Orgasm is dry (no semen). Younger men with good erections beforehand and nerve-sparing surgery do best.
  • External radiation: erections usually weaken gradually over years. Hormone therapy lowers sex drive and erections while you take it.
  • Brachytherapy: similar to external radiation, with gradual change over years.
  • Active surveillance and watchful waiting: only the changes of normal aging, unless treatment is needed later.

With every option, treatments for erection problems (pills, injections, devices and implants) are available and work for many men.

Bowel

  • External radiation: bowel urgency and looser stools are common during treatment and usually settle. In ProtecT, bloody stools were more common after radiation from the second year onward. At 12 years, about 12 in 100 men after radiation had some bowel leakage, compared with about 6 in 100 in the other groups. Modern techniques and rectal spacers aim to lower this.
  • Brachytherapy: lasting bowel problems are uncommon, but more likely when it is combined with external radiation.
  • Surgery: bowel function is not usually affected.
  • Active surveillance and watchful waiting: no effect.

Time, recovery and visits

  • Active surveillance: no recovery time. Regular PSA tests, and MRIs and biopsies every few years, for as long as you stay on surveillance.
  • Surgery: usually one night in hospital, a catheter for 1 to 2 weeks, 2 to 4 weeks off desk work and about 6 weeks before heavy lifting.
  • External radiation: 5 to about 28 weekday visits to the cancer centre, plus planning visits. Most men keep working. Hormone therapy, if needed, lasts months to years.
  • Brachytherapy: 1 or 2 short procedures, back to normal in a few days; longer if combined with external radiation.
  • Watchful waiting: occasional check-ups and PSA tests.

If the cancer comes back or grows

  • Active surveillance: all treatment options remain open if you need treatment later.
  • Surgery: a rising PSA is easy to spot, because it should be almost zero. Radiation, with or without hormone therapy, can then be given.
  • External radiation and brachytherapy: further treatment is possible, but surgery after radiation is more difficult and has more side effects.
  • Watchful waiting: hormone therapy and other medicines can control the cancer if it causes symptoms, but the aim is control rather than cure.

Part 4

What matters to you

Every option involves trade-offs. Think about how important each of these is to you. There are no right or wrong answers, and it is fine to talk them over with your partner or family.

Having the cancer treated now, so I know it has been dealt with
Keeping my erections and sex life as they are now
Avoiding leaking urine
Avoiding bowel problems
Avoiding hormone therapy and its side effects
Avoiding an operation and a general anaesthetic
Getting back to normal life quickly, with few hospital visits
Avoiding regular biopsies and waiting for test results
Keeping as many treatment choices as possible for later
