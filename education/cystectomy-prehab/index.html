Urology education

Getting ready for bladder removal surgery

A 4-week home program to help you go into your cystectomy as strong and prepared as you can be: moving more, breathing exercises, eating well, quitting smoking, looking after your mood, and knowing what to expect.

Start where you are. You don’t need a gym or special equipment. A daily walk, a kitchen chair and a few minutes of breathing practice are enough to begin. Doing something most days matters more than doing a lot.
It works alongside your care team. This program doesn’t replace the instructions you get from Dr. Power’s office or the Pre-Admission Clinic. If anything here is different from what they tell you, follow their instructions.

Part 1

Why prepare?

A radical cystectomy (removal of the bladder) is one of the biggest operations in urology. The weeks before it are a chance to build up your strength, so your body has more in reserve to heal.

Getting ready like this is often called prehabilitation, or “prehab”: rehabilitation that starts before surgery instead of after it. It usually combines exercise, good nutrition, breathing practice, stopping smoking and support for your mood.

Problems after this surgery are common, but most are minor

In a large US study of 203 people having cystectomy for bladder cancer, 178 could be fully followed up. Here is what happened to them in the first 30 days after surgery:

Any complication

About 60 in 100 people had at least one, such as a urine infection, slow return of bowel function or needing a blood transfusion.

A serious complication

About 12 in 100 had one that needed a procedure, another operation or intensive care.

Figures are rounded from both groups of the SWOG S1600 trial (Hamilton-Reeves and colleagues, 2026): 62% and 58% had any complication, and 11% and 13% a serious one. Your own risk depends on your health and the type of surgery. Ask Dr. Power what applies to you.

Two other things are worth knowing. About 1 in 6 people need to go back into hospital within a month of this surgery. And in large population studies, about 3 in 100 people died within 30 days of the operation and about 5 in 100 within 90 days. These rates vary between hospitals and with a person’s age and health.

People who are less fit, frail, underweight or poorly nourished, who smoke, or who drink a lot of alcohol tend to have more complications and a slower recovery. Some of these things can be improved, even in a few weeks.

What the research shows, honestly

  • Prehab has been safe in the trials so far, where most people were in their late 60s or 70s.
  • It builds strength and fitness. In a Danish trial of 107 people, those who exercised before (and after) surgery had stronger legs going into surgery and a year later, and got back to looking after themselves (washing, dressing, walking) about a day sooner.
  • It has not yet been shown to prevent complications or shorten the hospital stay after cystectomy. Only a few small trials have looked at this (5 trials, 282 people in a 2024 review), so we can’t yet say either way.
  • Stopping smoking before surgery is well proven to lower the risk of lung and wound problems after many kinds of operations.

So we can’t promise that prehab will prevent problems. But it is safe, it helps most people feel stronger and more in control, and the habits you build now are the same ones that will help you recover.

Don’t delay your surgery to finish this program. The timing of your operation matters, especially after chemotherapy. Start now and do what you can in the time you have.

Exercise safely

Stop and call 911 if you have chest pain or pressure, severe shortness of breath, or you faint, while exercising or at any other time.

Stop exercising and talk to your family doctor or our office if you feel dizzy or light-headed, your heart races or beats unevenly, or you have new pain in a joint that doesn’t settle with rest.

Check with your doctor before you start if you:

  • have had a heart attack or a heart stent in the last few months, or have chest pain that comes on more easily or more often than before;
  • have heart or lung disease that limits what you can do, or high blood pressure that isn’t under control;
  • take insulin or diabetes pills that can cause low blood sugar (such as gliclazide or glyburide), since exercise can lower your sugar;
  • have been told you are very anemic (low blood count), or have a lot of blood in your urine;
  • have fallen in the last year, or are getting chemotherapy (on low-energy days, a short gentle walk is enough).

While you wait for surgery: when to get help

  • Go to the emergency department if you can’t pass urine, or you are passing blood clots and it is hard to pass urine.
  • If you are having chemotherapy and get a fever, follow the fever instructions your chemotherapy team gave you. If you don’t have any, go to the emergency department.
  • Call our office at 1-888-909-2786 if you have more blood in your urine than usual, new pain in your side or back, or you are feeling much weaker.

Part 2

Your 4-week plan

Each week builds a little on the one before. Every day, aim to do four things: walk, practise your breathing, eat protein at each meal, and on 2 or 3 days, do your strength exercises.

  • Walk. At a pace where you can talk but not sing. Build up from about 10 minutes toward 30 minutes a day.
  • Strength exercises. On 2 or 3 days a week, not two days in a row, do the five home strength exercises.
  • Breathing practice. Deep breaths and a supported cough, twice a day. If you have a breathing trainer, use it too.
  • Protein at every meal. A protein food at breakfast, lunch and supper.
Surgery sooner or later than 4 weeks away?

Don’t delay your surgery to finish the program. Start now and do what you can.

Less time: start with Week 1 and move up as you feel able, but switch to the Week 4 “Get ready” goals for the last 7 days before surgery. Even a week or two of walking and breathing practice is worth doing. If you enter your dates below, the checklist marks those last 7 days for you.

More time, for example after chemotherapy: repeat Week 2 or 3 until the last week before surgery, then do Week 4.

Getting chemotherapy now: keep moving on the good days and rest on the bad ones. A short, gentle walk still counts. Avoid crowded gyms and pools when your care team says your infection-fighting blood cells are low.

This week, also:

Your self-check: Week 1 and Week 4

Two quick tests show how your strength and stamina change. Compare yourself only with yourself. Skip either test if it doesn’t feel safe for you.

How to do the 30-second chair stand
  1. Use a firm chair, ideally without arms, with its back against a wall so it can’t slide.
  2. Sit in the middle of the seat, feet flat on the floor, arms crossed over your chest.
  3. When you start a timer, stand up fully, then sit back down. Repeat as many times as you safely can in 30 seconds.
  4. Count the number of full stands. If you need to push with your hands, that’s fine: note “used hands”.
How to do the 6-minute walk
  1. Choose a flat, safe route: a long hallway, a mall, or a quiet sidewalk you can measure (for example, by counting houses or lamp posts).
  2. Walk as far as you can in 6 minutes at your own fastest comfortable pace. Don’t run. You may slow down or rest; the timer keeps going.
  3. Note how far you got: number of laps, houses or blocks, or the step count on your phone.
  4. Use the same route each time so you can compare.

Part 3

Move: walking, strength and breathing

Three kinds of exercise help most before surgery: something that makes you a bit out of breath (like walking), exercises that strengthen your legs and arms, and breathing practice.

1. Walking, or other “out of breath” exercise

Walking is the simplest and safest choice. Go outdoors, around a mall, or up and down a hallway. A stationary bike, swimming or water walking, or marching on the spot while holding a counter also count.

How hard? Use the talk test: you should be breathing faster and feel warm, but still able to talk in short sentences. If you can sing, speed up a little. If you can’t say a few words, slow down. It should feel “somewhat hard”, not exhausting.

  • Week 1: 10 to 20 minutes a day. Two 10-minute walks are fine.
  • Week 2: 20 to 30 minutes on at least 5 days.
  • Week 3: about 30 minutes on most days. If you feel well, try a few 1-minute stretches of faster walking, with 2 minutes of easy walking in between.
  • Week 4: keep walking 20 to 30 minutes a day at a comfortable pace. Just gentle walks in the last 2 days.

This builds toward Canada’s guideline for adults over 65: at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, plus strength exercises on at least 2 days.

2. Strength exercises at home

Strong legs help you get out of bed, onto the toilet and walking in the hallway after surgery. Do these five exercises on 2 or 3 days a week, not two days in a row.

  • Start with 1 set of 8 to 12 of each. Build up to 2 or 3 sets, with a minute’s rest between sets.
  • Work until you feel you could do only 2 or 3 more. Feeling a bit sore the next day is normal; pain in a joint is not.
  • Breathe out as you push or lift. Don’t hold your breath.

Sit to stand

Stand up from a firm chair and sit back down slowly. 8 to 12 times.

How to do it
  • Put the chair against a wall so it can’t slide. Sit near the front edge, feet flat and hip-width apart.
  • Lean forward a little (“nose over toes”) and stand up fully.
  • Sit back down slowly, taking about 3 seconds, without dropping into the chair.
  • Easier: push with your hands on the armrests or your thighs, or sit on a cushion.
  • Harder: fold your arms across your chest, use a lower chair, or hold a heavy book or bag at your chest.

Wall push-up

Push yourself away from a wall. 8 to 12 times.

How to do it
  • Stand an arm’s length from a wall. Put your hands flat on the wall at shoulder height, a little wider than your shoulders.
  • Keeping your body straight, bend your elbows and bring your chest toward the wall, then push back.
  • Harder: step your feet further back, or use a sturdy kitchen counter instead of the wall.

Step-ups

Step up onto the bottom stair and back down, holding the railing. 8 to 10 times on each leg.

How to do it
  • Hold the railing. Step up with your right foot, bring your left foot up beside it, then step down right, then left.
  • Do all your repeats leading with one leg, then switch.
  • Easier: use a lower step, or do heel raises instead.
  • Harder: do a few more repeats, or another set. Don’t rush; going slowly and steadily is safer.

Rows

Pull a weight or band toward your body to strengthen your back and arms. 10 to 12 times on each side.

How to do it
  • Hold a full water bottle or a can in one hand. Rest your other hand on a counter or the back of a sturdy chair, and lean forward a little with a straight back.
  • Let the weight hang down, then pull your elbow back past your side, squeezing your shoulder blade. Lower slowly.
  • Or sit tall and pull a resistance band (looped around a sturdy post or door handle) toward your belly with both hands.
  • Harder: a heavier bottle or a stronger band.

Heel raises

Rise up onto your toes while holding a counter. 10 to 15 times. Good for balance and calves.

How to do it
  • Stand facing a kitchen counter, holding on with both hands.
  • Slowly rise onto the balls of your feet, hold for a second, then lower slowly.
  • Harder: hold on with one hand, then with just your fingertips.

3. Breathing practice

After a big abdominal operation, it hurts to breathe deeply and cough, so the bottom parts of the lungs don’t open fully. This can lead to pneumonia. The nurses and physiotherapists will ask you to do breathing exercises 10 times every hour while you are awake after surgery. Learning them now makes them easier then.

  1. Deep breaths. Sit up straight with one hand on your belly. Breathe in slowly and deeply through your nose, so your belly pushes your hand out. Then breathe out slowly through pursed lips, as if blowing out a candle. Do this 5 to 10 times.
  2. Supported cough. Hold a pillow firmly against your belly (after surgery, over your incision). Take a deep breath and give 2 or 3 strong coughs. If you feel phlegm, try to cough it up. A “huff”, breathing out hard with your mouth open as if fogging a mirror, also works.

Before surgery, practise both twice a day.

Should I use a breathing trainer?

An inspiratory muscle trainer is a small hand-held device with an adjustable valve that makes it harder to breathe in, so it strengthens the muscles you breathe with. In studies of people having heart and major abdominal surgery, training with one for a few weeks before surgery lowered the chance of pneumonia and of parts of the lung collapsing. It has not been studied much before cystectomy specifically.

It is optional. Some pharmacies, medical supply stores and online stores sell them. If you get one:

  • Follow the device instructions. A common plan is 30 breaths, twice a day, sitting upright.
  • Choose a setting where the last few of the 30 breaths feel hard but you can still finish. Turn it up a little each week if it gets easy.
  • Stop if you feel dizzy, and rest between breaths if needed.
  • Ask your doctor before using one if you have ever had a collapsed lung, have a perforated eardrum, or have severe lung disease such as emphysema.

Blowing up balloons or breathing out through a straw are not the same thing and have not been shown to help.

Having a neobladder?

If your plan is a neobladder (a new bladder made from bowel, so you pass urine the usual way), you will rely on your pelvic floor muscles to stay dry afterward. Learning to find and squeeze these muscles before surgery may help. Ask the team whether to start now, and see our guide to pelvic floor exercises. If you are having a urostomy, you don’t need these exercises for bladder control.

Part 4

Eat well, and get checked

Your body uses protein to heal wounds, fight infection and keep your muscles. Going into surgery well nourished matters more than eating any special food.

Protein at every meal

The simplest rule: have a protein food at breakfast, lunch and supper, about the size of the palm of your hand, and a protein snack if you can.

  • 1 egg: about 6 g of protein
  • 1 cup (250 mL) of milk: about 9 g
  • ¾ cup (175 g) of Greek yogurt: about 15 g
  • ½ cup (125 mL) of cottage cheese: about 14 g
  • 50 g (1½ oz) of cheddar cheese: about 12 g
  • 75 g (2½ oz) of cooked chicken, fish, beef or pork: about 20 g
  • A small can of tuna or salmon, drained: about 20 to 25 g
  • ¾ cup (175 mL) of cooked lentils or beans: about 12 g
  • 2 tablespoons of peanut butter, or ¼ cup of nuts: about 7 g

Amounts are approximate, from Health Canada’s nutrient tables.

How much protein do I need?

Before major surgery, many programs aim for about 1.2 to 1.5 g of protein for every kilogram you weigh, each day. For someone who weighs 70 kg (154 lb), that is about 85 to 105 g a day: for example, 2 eggs and a glass of milk at breakfast (about 21 g), a tuna sandwich at lunch (about 30 g, counting the bread), a palm-sized piece of chicken and a glass of milk at supper (about 29 g), and a Greek yogurt as a snack (15 g). That adds up to about 95 g.

If you have kidney disease or have been told to limit protein, ask your doctor or a dietitian before eating more protein. Many people having cystectomy have some loss of kidney function.

Not hungry? Eat small amounts often, have your protein first, and drink milk or a nutrition drink (such as Ensure or Boost; the “high protein” kinds have more) between meals. If you have diabetes, ask which kind is right for you.

This is not the time to diet to lose weight, unless your surgeon has asked you to. Losing weight quickly can mean losing muscle.

When to ask for a dietitian

Ask your care team or family doctor to see a dietitian if:

  • you have lost weight without trying: about 5% of your weight or more in the last 3 to 6 months (for example, 4 kg or 9 lb if you weigh 80 kg or 176 lb);
  • you have been eating much less than usual for more than a week, because of poor appetite, nausea, chemotherapy or trouble swallowing; or
  • you are very thin, or not sure you are eating enough.

You can also call Health811 (dial 811) to talk to a registered dietitian for free. If you are a patient at the cancer centre (for example, you had chemotherapy there), its supportive care office is at 519-685-8622.

What about “immune-boosting” drinks and supplements?

Some websites recommend special drinks with added arginine, fish oil and nucleotides (sometimes called immunonutrition) for the days before surgery. A large trial in people having cystectomy, published in 2026, found that these drinks did not lower complications compared with an ordinary nutrition drink. In that trial, urine infections were actually more common with the special drink (32 in 100) than with the ordinary one (17 in 100). We don’t recommend buying them.

You don’t need high-dose vitamins or fish oil capsules either. Some supplements and herbal products can affect bleeding or the anaesthetic, so bring a list of everything you take to the Pre-Admission Clinic, and don’t start anything new without asking.

Things to ask your care team about

Some health problems can be improved in the weeks before surgery. Ask early, because some treatments take a few weeks to work.

  • Low blood count (anemia). Common with bladder cancer, because of bleeding, and after chemotherapy. It can make you tired and makes a blood transfusion more likely. Ask: “Is my hemoglobin low? Should my iron be checked?” If your iron is low, iron pills or an iron infusion can help. The Pre-Admission Clinic has a blood conservation nurse who may see you about this.
  • Diabetes. Good sugar control lowers the risk of infection. Ask: “Which diabetes medicines should I stop before surgery, and when?” (Some pills, such as empagliflozin (Jardiance) or dapagliflozin (Forxiga), and combination pills that contain them, are usually stopped a few days before surgery.) Also ask whether the sugary drinks before surgery are right for you.
  • Weekly diabetes or weight-loss injections, or pills like them. Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro) slow down how fast your stomach empties, which matters for the anaesthetic. Tell the Pre-Admission Clinic early if you take one; you will get specific instructions about stopping it or about eating and drinking before surgery.
  • Blood thinners (for example warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, clopidogrel). You will be told if and when to stop them. Don’t stop on your own.
  • Sleep apnea, heart or lung disease. Tell the Pre-Admission Clinic. Bring your CPAP machine to the hospital.
  • All your medicines. Bring them in their original containers, plus vitamins and herbal products, to your Pre-Admission Clinic visit. What to expect at the Pre-Admission Clinic (PDF).

Part 5

Smoking and alcohol

Stopping smoking and cutting down on alcohol are two of the most useful things you can do before surgery. Free help is available in Ontario, and nobody here will judge you for asking.

If you smoke

Smoking makes pneumonia, wound infections and slow healing more likely after surgery. Quitting at any point before surgery helps, and the longer before, the better: stopping at least 4 weeks before surgery lowers the risk of these problems more. Smoking is also a major cause of bladder cancer, and stopping is good for your heart, lungs and long-term health.

Your chances of quitting are much better with support and medicine than with willpower alone. Free help in Ontario:

  • Health811: dial 811 and ask for quit-smoking help. Quit coaches can talk with you by phone, mostly between 10 a.m. and 10 p.m., and can arrange free nicotine replacement by mail.
  • STOP on the Net: free nicotine patches plus gum or lozenges for about 10 weeks, mailed to you. Sign up at nicotinedependenceclinic.com.
  • Smokers’ Helpline (Canadian Cancer Society): online quit program, chat and text support. Visit smokershelpline.ca or text iQuit to 123456.
  • Talk Tobacco: free quit support for First Nations, Inuit and Métis people, 1-833-998-8255.
  • Prescription medicines: varenicline (Champix) and bupropion (Zyban) are covered by the Ontario Drug Benefit, which includes everyone 65 and older. Ask your family doctor or pharmacist.

Using a nicotine patch together with gum or lozenges for cravings works better than one alone. Nicotine replacement is much safer than smoking, including before surgery. If you vape or use cannabis, tell the anaesthesiologist. London Health Sciences Centre is smoke-free.

Alcohol

Regular drinking raises the risk of infection, bleeding, heart and lung problems and confusion after surgery. Cutting down or stopping for the 4 weeks before surgery may lower this risk.

Canada’s Guidance on Alcohol and Health says that 2 drinks or fewer a week is low risk. One standard drink is a 341 mL (12 oz) bottle of 5% beer, a 142 mL (5 oz) glass of 12% wine, or a 43 mL (1.5 oz) shot of 40% spirits.

Don’t stop suddenly if you drink heavily

If you drink every day, need a drink in the morning, or have had shakes, sweats or seeing things when you stopped before, stopping suddenly can be dangerous. Talk to your family doctor first about cutting down safely, and tell the Pre-Admission Clinic honestly how much you drink so the hospital can prevent withdrawal.

Free, confidential help: ConnexOntario, 1-866-531-2600, any time of day, connects you with alcohol and drug services near you.

Part 6

Look after your mind

Feeling worried, low or overwhelmed while waiting for cancer surgery is normal. A bladder cancer diagnosis, a big operation and changes to how you pass urine are a lot to take in.

Things that help

  • Know what to expect. Not knowing is often the hardest part. Read Part 7, and keep a running list of questions for your surgeon and the ostomy nurse. No question is too small.
  • Keep moving. A daily walk is one of the best things for worry and low mood, as well as for your body.
  • Practise relaxing. Slow breathing (breathe in for a count of 4, out for a count of 6) for a few minutes, or a guided relaxation recording. Try it at the same time each day, such as before bed.
  • Protect your sleep. Get up at the same time each day, get outside in daylight, avoid caffeine after lunch and alcohol in the evening, and put screens away for the last hour before bed.
  • Let people help. Bring someone to appointments to take notes. Accept offers of meals, rides and help around the house.
  • Talk to someone who has been there. Many people find it reassuring to talk with someone who has a urostomy or neobladder and is living well with it.

Free support

  • Bladder Cancer Canada: 1-866-674-8889, bladdercancercanada.org. Ask to talk with a trained volunteer who has had bladder cancer. They also run a free monthly online and phone support group about living with a urinary diversion, hosted by ostomy nurses, that patients and family can join.
  • Canadian Cancer Society Cancer Information Helpline: 1-888-939-3333. Information about cancer and about support services near you.
  • Social workers at the cancer centre can help with worry, family concerns, money and work worries, and practical problems. If you are a patient at the cancer centre, call supportive care at 519-685-8622 or ask your care team. Otherwise, your family doctor can help or refer you.

When to get help for your mood

Tell your family doctor or care team if, for 2 weeks or more, you have felt down or hopeless most days, lost interest in things you usually enjoy, or felt so anxious that it is hard to sleep, eat or get through the day. These feelings are common and treatable.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, get help now: call or text 988 (Suicide Crisis Helpline, any time), call or text Reach Out at 519-433-2023 (London and Middlesex, any time), or call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Part 7

Your surgery and recovery

London Health Sciences Centre uses an enhanced recovery plan (often called ERAS) for cystectomy. It is a set of steps, before and after surgery, that helps you get back to eating, walking and looking after yourself sooner. You are an important part of the team.

The operation

The surgeon removes your bladder and nearby lymph nodes. In men, the prostate is usually removed too. In women, the uterus, ovaries and part of the vagina are often removed; this depends on the cancer, so ask what applies to you. The surgeon then makes a new way for urine to leave your body, called a urinary diversion, using a piece of your bowel. Dr. Power will talk with you about which type suits you.

Urostomy (ileal conduit)

The most common type. Urine drains through a small opening on your belly, called a stoma, into a pouch you empty several times a day.

What it means for you
  • A short piece of the small bowel (the ileum) carries urine from the kidneys to the stoma, usually on the right side of your belly.
  • The pouch sticks to the skin and is hidden under clothes. You empty it into the toilet and change it every few days. At night you can connect it to a larger bag.
  • It is a shorter, simpler operation than a neobladder, with fewer things to manage afterward.
  • People with a urostomy swim, travel, work and exercise. It takes some weeks to get used to.

Neobladder

A new bladder made from a longer piece of bowel, joined to the urethra (the tube you pass urine through), so you pass urine the usual way. No outside bag.

What it means for you
  • Not everyone can have one. It depends on where the cancer is, your kidney function and your general health.
  • You go home with catheters (tubes) that drain the new bladder and need flushing twice a day for a few weeks. You will be taught how.
  • Afterward you pass urine on a schedule, by relaxing your pelvic floor and gently tightening your belly, because the neobladder doesn’t give the same “full” feeling.
  • Leaking, especially at night, is common in the first months and often improves. Some people need to empty it with a catheter.

A third type, a pouch inside the body that you empty through a small opening with a catheter, is used for a few people.

Meet the ostomy nurse

Before surgery, a nurse who specializes in wound, ostomy and continence care (an NSWOC, sometimes called an ET nurse) from the hospital’s Skin, Wound and Ostomy Team will meet you, often at your Pre-Admission Clinic visit. They:

  • explain what living with a urostomy or neobladder is like, and answer your questions;
  • mark the best place for a stoma on your belly, checking it while you sit, stand and bend, so the pouch fits well and you can see it. This is often done even if a neobladder is planned, in case a urostomy is needed;
  • teach you, and your family if you wish, how to empty and change your pouch or flush your catheters, starting in hospital.

If you haven’t heard about this visit by about 2 weeks before surgery, ask Dr. Power’s office.

The days before surgery

  • Pre-Admission Clinic. A visit or phone call days to weeks before surgery, sometimes taking most of a day. You may have blood tests and an ECG, and see an anaesthesiologist. Pre-Admission Clinic information (PDF).
  • Bowel preparation. Most people don’t need to drink a bowel “clean-out” before this surgery. You will be told if you do.
  • Don’t shave, wax or clip any body hair near the surgery area. It raises the risk of infection.
  • Eating and drinking. You don’t need to fast for long. In recent LHSC instructions, you could eat solid food until midnight and drink clear fluids (ones you can see through, such as water, apple juice, black tea or coffee) until 2 to 3 hours before surgery.
  • Sugary drinks. You may be asked to drink a clear sugary drink (such as apple juice, cranberry cocktail, iced tea or a sports drink) at bedtime the night before and again a few hours before surgery. It makes the wait before surgery more comfortable (less thirst, hunger and anxiety) and helps your body handle sugar better after surgery. If you have diabetes, ask what is right for you.
  • Medicines. Take only the ones you were told to take on the morning of surgery, with a sip of water.

Always follow the exact times and instructions the Pre-Admission Clinic gives you, since they can change. If you are unsure, call them: Victoria Hospital Pre-Admission Clinic, 519-685-8500 ext. 55960.

In hospital

The usual stay is about 5 to 7 days. Each day you will be asked to do a bit more. Getting up and moving prevents pneumonia, blood clots and weakness, and helps your bowel start working again.

  • The evening of surgery: sips of fluid starting about 2 hours after surgery, then a light meal if you feel like it. Sit up and dangle your legs at the side of the bed, or sit in a chair for a short time. Start your breathing and leg exercises.
  • Day 1: sit in the chair for every meal. Walk in the hallway at least 3 times. Chew gum 3 times a day for at least 5 minutes; it helps your bowel wake up. Breathing exercises 10 times every hour while awake. Look at your stoma or catheters.
  • Days 2 and 3: walk 3 to 5 times a day. Wash or shower. Start emptying your pouch, or flushing your catheters, with the nurse’s help.
  • Days 4 to 7: walk on your own, care for your stoma or catheters more independently, change a full pouch with help, and plan your ride home.
Tubes, pain control and other things you may notice
  • Tubes: an IV in your arm; two thin tubes (stents) coming out through the stoma or belly that drain each kidney while the joins heal. They are removed either in hospital or at a clinic visit about 10 to 14 days after surgery; often a small drain in your belly. With a neobladder, you also have catheters from the urethra and the belly.
  • Pain control: you may have an epidural (a thin tube in your back) or a pain pump you press yourself (only you should press the button), and regular pain pills. Tell your nurse if pain stops you from moving, breathing deeply or coughing. Aim to keep it below 4 out of 10.
  • Blood clot prevention: a daily injection of blood thinner, and air-filled sleeves on your legs at first. Injections often continue at home for some weeks.
  • Leg exercises every hour while you are in bed: straighten your legs, point and flex your feet, and circle your ankles.
  • Eating: eat as much or as little as you feel like, and sit in the chair for meals. Tell your nurse if you feel sick or bloated; medicine can help.
  • Rooms may be shared, and the hospital is scent-free.

At home

  • A home care nurse, arranged through Ontario Health atHome before you leave hospital, will visit to help with your stoma or neobladder.
  • Keep walking every day and increase gradually. Tiredness is normal for several weeks; rest when you need to.
  • For 4 to 6 weeks (or as your surgeon advises): don’t lift more than 5 to 10 lb (2 to 4 kg), and avoid shovelling, raking, vacuuming, sit-ups and hard exercise.
  • Don’t drive while you are taking opioid pain medicine (such as hydromorphone or oxycodone).
  • Keep eating protein, and drink about 8 to 10 glasses of fluid a day unless you have been told to limit fluids.
  • Ostomy supplies: the ostomy nurse will tell you where to buy them. Ontario’s Assistive Devices Program pays a yearly grant toward supplies for a permanent urostomy, or for a continent pouch you empty with a catheter: currently $975 a year, paid in two instalments, or $1,300 a year if you are on Ontario Works or ODSP or live in a long-term care home. The nurse can help you apply. Private insurance may cover more.

What to bring to the hospital

  • Your Ontario health card, hospital card and insurance information
  • All your medicines in their original containers, and an up-to-date list including vitamins and supplements
  • Glasses, hearing aids and dentures, in cases labelled with your name
  • Non-slip slippers or shoes, a housecoat and loose, comfortable clothes for going home
  • Unscented toothbrush, toothpaste, deodorant, lip balm and hairbrush (the hospital is scent-free)
  • Your cane, walker or CPAP machine, if you use one, labelled with your name
  • 2 or 3 packs of chewing gum
  • A few non-perishable snacks (crackers, cookies, pudding cups or cereal)
  • Your phone and a long charging cable, earplugs, and something to read
  • Your list of questions

Leave at home: jewellery (including rings), valuables and large amounts of money. Remove nail polish and body piercings before you come.

After surgery: when to get help

Call 911 if you have chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, you cough up blood, you faint, or you (or your family) notice new confusion.

Go to the emergency department, at the hospital where you had surgery if you can get there safely, if you have:

  • a fever of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher, or shaking chills;
  • no urine, or much less urine than usual, from your stoma or catheters;
  • a neobladder catheter that stops draining, and flushing it the way you were taught doesn’t clear it;
  • vomiting, or a swollen, hard belly.

Call your surgeon’s office (1-888-909-2786) the same day, or go to the emergency department if the office is closed or you feel very unwell, if you have:

  • new pain in your side or back (over your kidneys);
  • nausea that doesn’t go away, or pain your medicine doesn’t control;
  • an incision that is red, swollen, more painful, opening up, or draining pus or a bad smell;
  • a stoma that turns very pale, grey, purple or black;
  • bright red blood from inside the stoma or from your bottom;
  • pain or swelling in one leg (don’t rub it);
  • dizziness, a very dry mouth, great thirst or unusual tiredness, which can be signs of dehydration.
