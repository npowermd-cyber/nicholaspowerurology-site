Chronic pelvic pain in men

A short guide to chronic prostatitis and chronic pelvic pain syndrome: what it is, why tests are often normal, and how treatment is matched to your symptoms.

Your pain is real, common and treatable. About 1 in 12 men have these symptoms at any one time. Most men improve with the right mix of treatments, usually over several months.
Normal tests are good news. They mean an infection, cancer or damage is very unlikely. In many men the pain comes from tight pelvic floor muscles and a nervous system that has become extra sensitive.
Treatment is matched to you. No single pill fixes this. The men who do best combine a few treatments that fit their own symptoms, with daily self-care at the centre.

Part 1

What it is

Chronic pelvic pain syndrome means pain or discomfort in the pelvic area for at least 3 of the last 6 months, when tests have not found an infection, a stone, a tumour or another clear cause.

You may hear it called chronic prostatitis or CP/CPPS (chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome). Despite the name, the prostate is usually not infected, and often it is not the source of the pain at all.

Where it hurts

The pain often moves around and changes from day to day. Common places are:

  • between the scrotum and the anus (the perineum), often described as “sitting on a ball”;
  • the testicles, the penis, or the tip of the penis;
  • low in the belly, above the pubic bone;
  • the rectum, tailbone, groin, inner thighs or low back.

What often comes with it

  • Bladder: peeing often, urgency, getting up at night, a weak or stop-start stream, burning.
  • Sex: pain during or after ejaculation, changes in erections, less interest because of fear of pain.
  • Bowels: constipation, straining, pain with bowel movements.
  • Sitting: pain that builds the longer you sit.
  • Mood and sleep: worry, low mood and poor sleep, which are a normal response to pain and can also make it louder.

What causes it

For most men, there isn’t one single cause. Two things usually work together:

  • Tight pelvic floor muscles. The pelvic floor is the sling of muscles at the bottom of the pelvis. In this condition it is usually too tight, not too weak, a bit like a fist clenched for months. Tight, tender muscles can send pain to the penis, testicles, bladder and rectum, even though those organs are healthy.
  • A sensitive alarm system. When pain goes on for months, the nerves and brain get better at producing it, like a smoke alarm that goes off when you make toast. Stress, poor sleep and worry turn the sensitivity up. That does not mean the pain is “in your head”. It means the nervous system is involved, which also gives you ways to calm it.

Things that often start or feed the problem include a stressful period, an infection or painful procedure that taught the pelvis to “guard”, long hours sitting or cycling, constipation and straining, and heavy lifting with breath-holding. Pain that is milder in the morning, builds with sitting, flares in stressful weeks and eases on holiday is typical of muscle and nerve pain, not of infection or cancer.

More in the guide: Meet your pelvic floor, The cycle that keeps pain going and A sensitive alarm system.

Other names, and the four kinds of prostatitis

In letters and reports you may also see “category III prostatitis” or “primary prostate pain syndrome”. These mean the same thing as chronic pelvic pain syndrome.

Doctors sort prostatitis into four kinds, using a system from the US National Institutes of Health (NIH). Only the first two are infections.

  • Category I, acute bacterial prostatitis: a sudden, serious infection with fever, chills, pain and trouble peeing. It needs urgent antibiotics. See urgent help.
  • Category II, chronic bacterial prostatitis: repeated infections with the same germ, found on urine or prostate fluid tests. Treated with a longer course of the right antibiotic.
  • Category III, chronic pelvic pain syndrome: pelvic pain without a proven infection. This is by far the most common kind, and the subject of this page.
  • Category IV, “silent” inflammation: inflammation found by chance on a test, with no symptoms. It usually needs no treatment.

Part 2

Tests, and why they are often normal

There is no single test that proves this condition. The diagnosis is made from your symptoms and examination, once other causes have been checked for.

What your doctor usually checks

  • Your story and a symptom questionnaire. Where it hurts, what makes it better or worse, and how your bladder, bowels, sex life, mood and sleep are. Guidelines suggest a scored questionnaire such as the NIH Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index, which you can fill in below.
  • An examination. The belly, groin, testicles and penis, and a gentle rectal exam with a gloved finger to feel the prostate and the pelvic floor muscles around it. Tight, tender muscles that reproduce your usual pain are an important clue.
  • Urine tests. A urine test and urine culture (to grow any bacteria), and sometimes tests for sexually transmitted infections.
  • Sometimes more. Depending on your age and symptoms: a PSA blood test, a bladder scan to see whether you empty fully, a urine flow test, an ultrasound of the scrotum, or a test of urine before and after prostate massage if a chronic infection is suspected. A camera test of the bladder (cystoscopy) or a scan is used when the diagnosis is unclear or there is a warning sign such as blood in the urine.
Conditions your doctor rules out
  • Infection: urinary infection, bacterial prostatitis, sexually transmitted infections.
  • Blockage: an enlarged prostate, a narrowing of the urethra (stricture), a bladder that doesn’t empty.
  • Cancer: prostate, bladder or testicle cancer. This is uncommon, but blood in the urine, a lump in a testicle or a high PSA are always checked.
  • Other bladder problems: bladder stones, or bladder pain syndrome (interstitial cystitis).
  • Nerves, bones and joints: a pinched pudendal nerve, problems in the low back, hips or pelvic joints, or a hernia.
  • Bowel: hemorrhoids, a tear (fissure), or other bowel conditions.

Normal results are good news

Standard tests look for infection, blockage and cancer. They cannot measure a tight muscle or a sensitive nerve, so they are usually normal in this condition. That tells us what you almost certainly don’t have.

Once the right tests have been done, more tests rarely add answers, and can turn up harmless findings that cause more worry. You should be checked again if your symptoms change in an important way or you notice a warning sign.

What about antibiotics?

When urine and other cultures are negative, repeated courses of antibiotics don’t help, and they bring side effects such as upset bowels and tendon problems. Guidelines advise against them.

Some doctors offer one course early on, if symptoms are less than a year old and you haven’t had antibiotics for them before. If it doesn’t clearly help, more courses won’t.

If a test does find bacteria, that is a different condition (bacterial prostatitis), and the antibiotic is chosen from the test result.

Part 3

Treatment matched to you

This condition is different in every man, so there is no one treatment for everyone. Current guidelines recommend combining several treatments chosen to fit your main symptoms.

Many urologists use a simple checklist called UPOINT to do this. Each letter is a group of symptoms: Urinary, Psychological, Organ (the prostate), Infection, Nerves and Tender muscles. Most men have two or three groups, and each group has its own treatments. Read the groups below and notice which sound like you. Then ask your doctor which treatments fit.

The foundation for almost everyone

  • Understanding the pain. Knowing that hurt does not mean harm, and that flares pass, lowers fear and tension. It is part of treatment.
  • Daily relaxation practice for the body, the breath and the pelvic floor. See Self-care.
  • Pelvic floor physiotherapy if the muscles are tight and tender. See clinics.
  • Changing the habits that feed tension: long sitting, constipation and straining, breath-holding when lifting.

It is normal to try a treatment for a set time, often 6 to 12 weeks, then keep what helps and stop what doesn’t.

U

Urinary

Peeing often, urgency, getting up at night, a weak or stop-start stream, or a feeling of not emptying.

What can help
  • Bladder habits: drink water steadily through the day, cut back on bladder irritants (such as coffee, alcohol, fizzy drinks and spicy food) to see which matter for you, avoid going “just in case”, and don’t push to pee.
  • An alpha-blocker (tamsulosin, alfuzosin or silodosin) relaxes the muscle at the bladder outlet. It may help most when the stream is weak and symptoms are fairly recent, though trials have had mixed results. Side effects can include dizziness on standing and less semen at ejaculation. If you ever need cataract surgery, tell your eye surgeon you take (or have taken) an alpha-blocker, because it can affect the iris during the operation.
  • Daily tadalafil, a medicine also used for erections and prostate enlargement, is an option for some men. Never take it with nitrate heart medicines (such as nitroglycerin spray or patches), because together they can drop your blood pressure dangerously.
  • If the prostate is enlarged, a medicine that shrinks it may be added.

P

Psychological and social

Worry about what the pain means, low mood, stress, feeling hopeless, or pain taking over work and relationships.

What can help
  • This does not mean the pain is imaginary. Worry and stress feed the cycle of muscle tension and nerve sensitivity, so treating them has a physical effect.
  • Pain psychology: cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), mindfulness programs and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) teach practical skills. Guidelines recommend referral when distress is significant.
  • Cutting back on internet searching and body checking. Many men notice a difference from this alone.
  • Involving your partner or family. Sharing the guide can help.

If you ever feel hopeless or think about harming yourself, call or text 9-8-8 (Suicide Crisis Helpline, 24 hours a day across Canada), tell your doctor, or go to an emergency department.

O

Prostate

The prostate itself is tender on examination, or tests show inflammation in the prostate fluid.

What can help
  • Anti-inflammatories such as ibuprofen or naproxen, for short periods or during flares. Check with your doctor or pharmacist that they are safe for you.
  • Plant-based supplements: quercetin and rye pollen extract have some evidence from small trials and few side effects. Saw palmetto has not shown lasting benefit for this condition. Ask your pharmacist about interactions with your other medicines.

I

Infection

Bacteria have been found on a urine or prostate fluid test, or you have had infections proven by culture.

What can help
  • An antibiotic chosen from the culture result, usually for several weeks.
  • If cultures have been negative, this group doesn’t apply, and repeated antibiotics won’t help.

N

Nerves and whole body

Burning or electric pain, pain to light touch, pain beyond the pelvis, or other pain conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia or chronic headaches.

What can help
  • Nerve-calming medicines such as low-dose amitriptyline or nortriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin. They start at a low dose, go up slowly, and take several weeks to work. They are used for their effect on nerves, and often help sleep.
  • Regular gentle exercise, good sleep and relaxation practice, which all calm a sensitive nervous system.
  • A TENS machine (a small device that sends mild electrical pulses through skin pads) may ease pain.
  • If pain stays severe, referral to a chronic pain program. In London, the Pain Management Program at St. Joseph’s Health Care London needs a referral from your family doctor or nurse practitioner.

T

Tender muscles

Tight, tender pelvic floor or hip muscles on examination, often reproducing your usual pain. This is one of the most common groups.

What can help
  • Pelvic floor physiotherapy: hands-on release of tight muscles inside and outside the pelvis, and down-training: practising how to let the pelvic floor drop and relax on purpose. This sometimes uses biofeedback, where a small sensor shows your muscle tension on a screen so you can see when it lets go. Treatment also includes breathing, stretching and a home program. See clinics near London.
  • A daily relaxation practice and gentle stretches. See Learning to let go in the guide.
  • Avoid Kegel (squeezing) exercises unless a pelvic floor physiotherapist has assessed you and recommends them. They train already-tight muscles to tighten more.
  • For muscles that don’t respond, a specialist may offer trigger point injections (a small amount of numbing medicine injected into a tight, tender knot in the muscle) or, rarely, Botox injections into the pelvic floor, usually to make physiotherapy easier.

S

Sexual

Pain with or after ejaculation, erection problems, or ejaculating too soon. European guidelines add this group to UPOINT.

What can help
  • Knowing that sex and ejaculation do not cause damage. Pain after ejaculation comes from the pelvic floor clenching and not letting go. A warm bath or relaxation practice afterward helps.
  • Pelvic floor physiotherapy, which can improve both pain and sexual function.
  • Erection medicines such as tadalafil or sildenafil, if erections are a problem. Never take them with nitrate heart medicines (such as nitroglycerin).
  • Taking the pressure off for a while, and talking openly with your partner. A sex therapist can help couples.

Other treatments worth asking about

These can help across several groups and are worth asking about.

  • Acupuncture. Several trials found real acupuncture worked better than sham (fake) acupuncture, and the benefit often lasted after the course ended. It takes a course of regular sessions over several weeks. Not covered by OHIP; some extended health plans cover it.
  • Low-intensity shockwave therapy to the perineum. Trials show it lowers symptom scores, with few side effects. When the trials were pooled for the American Urological Association guideline, men’s NIH-CPSI scores at the end of treatment were on average about 8 points lower than in the comparison groups. It is offered at some private clinics and is not covered by OHIP.
  • Regular aerobic exercise such as walking or swimming.

What doesn’t help

  • Repeated courses of antibiotics when cultures are negative.
  • Surgery on the prostate to relieve pain. It doesn’t treat the muscles and nerves, and it can make things worse.
  • Long-term steroid tablets.
  • Opioid pain medicines. Over time they can make pain sensitivity worse and cause constipation.
  • Kegel strengthening exercises, unless a physiotherapist recommends them after an assessment.
  • Searching for one magic cure. Men who do best combine several modest helps, practised every day.

More in the guide: Medications and other treatments.

How strong is the evidence?

Most treatments for this condition have been tested in small trials, so guidelines often make “may be offered” rather than firm recommendations. In short:

  • Moderate support: low-intensity shockwave therapy (several small trials, mostly outside North America, with consistent benefit).
  • Moderate support, mixed trials: alpha-blockers for men with urinary symptoms. Pooled results show a modest benefit, but some trials, including one in recently diagnosed men, found no benefit over placebo. Acupuncture: a large trial showed benefit, and guidelines differ on how strongly to recommend it.
  • Reasonable options with limited evidence: anti-inflammatories, quercetin and rye pollen extract, daily tadalafil, nerve-calming medicines, CBT, TENS, and hands-on pelvic floor physiotherapy. Physiotherapy trials are small, but it is recommended first for tight, tender muscles and is low risk.
  • Did not work in the main trial: pregabalin did not reach its main goal in a large trial, though it may help some men.

Part 4

Self-care that makes a difference

The most powerful treatments are the ones you practise yourself, every day. Here is the short version. The Releasing Pelvic Pain guide explains each step in detail.

Relax on purpose, daily One 20 to 30 minute session a day of slow belly breathing, letting the pelvic floor soften and drop, and a body scan (moving your attention slowly through the body and letting each part soften). Add short “where am I holding on?” check-ins through the day. How to do it
Sit less, sit better Get up every 30 to 45 minutes. Try a cushion with a cut-out that takes pressure off the perineum. Stop every hour on long drives. Everyday habits
Keep bowels soft Fibre, water and a footstool on the toilet. Don’t strain or sit there for long. Constipation is one of the most overlooked causes of pelvic pain.
Calm the bladder Drink water steadily, find your own bladder irritants, and avoid going “just in case”. When urgency hits, stand still and breathe slowly until it passes.
Move gently Walking, swimming and stretching usually help. Cycling, heavy lifting with breath-holding and lots of sit-ups often aggravate symptoms for a while. Breathe out when you lift.
Protect sleep and calm worry Regular sleep times, less late-night searching online, and time for things you enjoy. Calming the alarm system
Use warmth A warm bath or a warm pack on the perineum or lower belly eases tight muscles, especially before your relaxation practice or after sex.
Have a flare plan Flares are part of recovery. They are not damage and they pass. Write your plan when you feel well. Handling flare-ups

Most men start to notice change within 2 to 3 months of steady practice and physiotherapy, and keep improving for 6 to 12 months. Recovery is rarely a straight line. Measure it over weeks, not days. The symptom tracker below can help.

Part 5

Pelvic floor physiotherapy clinics

Clinics in London and southwestern Ontario that offer pelvic floor physiotherapy. In Ontario you can book with a physiotherapist yourself. You don’t need a doctor’s referral, although some insurance plans ask for one.

Before you call

  • Ask whether they treat men with pelvic pain and a tight (overactive) pelvic floor. Some clinics focus mainly on pregnancy, childbirth and women’s health.
  • Ask whether the assessment includes an internal (rectal) exam of the pelvic floor muscles, and whether treatment includes muscle release and relaxation training. You can always ask to slow down, stop, or leave the internal exam for another visit.
  • A program built mainly on strengthening (Kegels, or machines that make the muscles contract) is usually not what a tight, painful pelvic floor needs.

Cost

  • Private physiotherapy is usually not covered by OHIP. Many extended health plans cover part of the cost. Ask the clinic about fees and direct billing.
  • You may qualify for OHIP-funded physiotherapy at some community clinics if you are 65 or older, had a hospital stay or day surgery in the last 12 months for a condition that needs physiotherapy, or receive Ontario Works or ODSP. Not all of these clinics offer pelvic floor care.
  • If physiotherapy isn’t possible for you, the daily self-care is still very worthwhile on its own.

Part 6

Track your symptoms

The NIH Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index (NIH-CPSI) is the questionnaire urologists use most to measure these symptoms. Fill it in now, then again every few weeks, to see how things are changing.

  • It takes about 2 minutes. All questions are about the last week.
  • It gives three scores: pain (0 to 21), urinary (0 to 10) and quality of life impact (0 to 12), adding up to a total (0 to 43). Lower is better.
  • Bring your scores to appointments. They help you and your care team judge whether a treatment is working.

Prefer paper? Print the score sheet (PDF, 2 pages), with the questions, a scoring guide and a log for your scores.

Your answers stay on this device. Nothing is sent to the office or anyone else. Saved scores are kept only in this browser so you can compare over time. On a shared computer, use “Clear all my scores” when you finish.

Pain or discomfort
1. In the last week, have you experienced any pain or discomfort in the following areas?
a. Area between rectum and testicles (perineum)
b. Testicles
c. Tip of the penis (not related to urination)
d. Below your waist, in your pubic or bladder area
2. In the last week, have you experienced:
a. Pain or burning during urination?
b. Pain or discomfort during or after sexual climax (ejaculation)?
3. How often have you had pain or discomfort in any of these areas over the last week?
4. Which number best describes your AVERAGE pain or discomfort on the days that you had it, over the last week?
No painPain as bad as you can imagine
Urination
5. How often have you had a sensation of not emptying your bladder completely after you finished urinating, over the last week?
6. How often have you had to urinate again less than two hours after you finished urinating, over the last week?
Impact of symptoms
7. How much have your symptoms kept you from doing the kinds of things you would usually do, over the last week?
8. How much did you think about your symptoms, over the last week?
Quality of life
9. If you were to spend the rest of your life with your symptoms just the way they have been during the last week, how would you feel about that?

To add up your scores, see “Scoring it by hand” below.

Scoring it by hand, and what the scores mean
  • Pain score (0 to 21): add questions 1a, 1b, 1c, 1d, 2a and 2b (1 point for each Yes), plus question 3 (Never 0 to Always 5) and question 4 (the number you chose).
  • Urinary score (0 to 10): add questions 5 and 6 (Not at all 0 to Almost always 5).
  • Quality of life impact score (0 to 12): add questions 7 and 8 (None 0 to A lot 3) and question 9 (Delighted 0 to Terrible 6).
  • Total (0 to 43): add the three scores.

What counts as a real change? In studies, a drop of 6 points or more in the total score was the change that best matched men saying they felt better. This rule of thumb comes from men who started with moderate or severe symptoms; if your first score is low, smaller changes can still matter. Ups and downs from week to week are normal. Look at the trend over a month or more. A clear rise of 6 points or more is a good reason to contact your care team.

The score measures how much your symptoms bother you. It does not make a diagnosis, and it can’t tell you about another condition. If your score rises sharply, or you have any warning signs, contact your doctor.

The NIH-CPSI was developed by the Chronic Prostatitis Collaborative Research Network of the US National Institutes of Health (Litwin MS and colleagues, Journal of Urology, 1999). The questions are reproduced here exactly as published.

Part 7

When to get help urgently

Chronic pelvic pain is not dangerous, but it doesn’t protect you from other problems. Don’t assume every new symptom is part of your usual pain.

Go to an emergency department now if you have

  • Fever of 38°C or higher, or shaking chills, with pelvic pain, burning or trouble peeing. This can be a serious infection of the prostate (acute bacterial prostatitis) or kidney that needs antibiotics the same day.
  • You can’t pass urine, or only dribble with a full, painful bladder.
  • Severe pain deep in the perineum or rectum with fever, which can mean a collection of pus in the prostate (abscess) or another serious infection.
  • Redness, swelling or darkening skin that spreads quickly, or severe pain, in the scrotum, penis or perineum, especially if you have diabetes. This can be a rare but life-threatening skin and tissue infection.
  • Sudden, severe pain or swelling in a testicle. A twisted testicle needs surgery within hours.
  • Numbness in the groin, buttocks or genitals, new weakness in your legs, or new loss of control of your bladder or bowels.

If you feel very unwell, are confused, or can’t get to hospital safely, call 9-1-1.

Call our office or your family doctor soon if you notice

  • blood in your urine that you can see;
  • blood in your semen that is new or keeps happening;
  • a new lump or swelling in a testicle, the scrotum or the groin;
  • bleeding from the rectum, or a lasting change in your bowel habits;
  • weight loss you can’t explain, or drenching night sweats;
  • pain that is clearly different from your usual pattern, or steadily getting worse;
  • new burning when you pee, or a new discharge from the penis, which can be signs of an infection that needs a urine test or swab.

If you are struggling emotionally

Living with pain can be exhausting. If you feel hopeless or have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 9-8-8 (Suicide Crisis Helpline, 24 hours a day across Canada) or go to an emergency department.
